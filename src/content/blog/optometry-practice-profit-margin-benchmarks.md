Optometry practice profit margin benchmarks become useful when you know what each source counts as profit. Published references use different measures: some include doctor compensation, while others report earnings after that expense.

Comparing those figures without checking their definitions can lead an owner to draw the wrong conclusion about the practice.

For the owner who does the books, the practical starting point is a clearly labeled financial statement. Identify the revenue being measured, the expenses deducted, and the treatment of owner and associate compensation. Then choose a benchmark that matches.

This guide brings the published figures together and explains how to use them in a financial review, whether you run a solo office or a group.

## What Published Optometry Profit Margin Benchmarks Measure

### Recent practice data and lender reference ranges

The following figures describe different financial measures. Keep the metric name attached to the percentage whenever you use it in a worksheet or management report.

| Source | Measure | Published benchmark | Context for comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| IDOC’s 2025 benchmarks, reported by Optometry Times | Doctor compensation plus EBITDA, described as “practice net” | Median of 31% of collections | Includes doctor pay; the sample consists of IDOC bookkeeping clients and skews larger than average |
| IDOC’s 2025 benchmarks, reported by Optometry Times | Unadjusted EBITDA | Median of 16% of revenue | A separate measure from doctor compensation plus EBITDA |
| Vision One Credit Union | Pre-tax net income | Typical range of 5%–25% | Preserve the pre-tax net-income definition |
| Vision One Credit Union | Normalized cash flow | Typical range of 15%–30% | Review the normalization assumptions before comparing |

Sources: [Optometry Times’ report on IDOC benchmarks](https://www.optometrytimes.com/view/independent-od-practices-net-31-of-revenue-idoc-benchmarks-show) and [Vision One Credit Union’s Loan FAQs](https://www.visionone.org/resources/loan-faqs).

The distinction within the IDOC report deserves particular attention. Its combined doctor-pay-and-profit measure is not interchangeable with its unadjusted EBITDA measure. Also, the bookkeeping-client sample should not be treated as a universal target for every practice.

Vision One likewise separates “Net income (pre-tax)” from “Normalized cash flow.” Carry that distinction into your own reporting rather than placing both figures under a generic profit heading.

### Historical guidance and individual practice targets

A 2019 *Optometric Management* article suggests a **25%–35% margin** for traditional optometry practices.

Its definition explicitly says, “Net income includes all financial benefits of any kind provided to doctors.” That includes compensation and benefits, making the figure different from profit calculated after doctor compensation.

Treat it as historical guidance with a specific definition. [Software for Financial Health](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2019/november/software-for-financial-health).

A *Review of Optometric Business* article describes targets used in an individual growing practice: **15%–18% for OD compensation** and **4%–17% for profit or cash flow**. These are the author’s operating targets, with an explanation that practice circumstances affect them.

They are not presented as a national median. [Practice expense targets in Review of Optometric Business](https://reviewob.com/metrics-we-used-to-grow-our-optometry-practice-10x).

The 2015 *Financial Management Planner: Success Points* reports a historical median practice cash-flow ratio of **30% before OD compensation**.

It distinguishes that measure from cash flow after OD compensation, a useful distinction when reviewing a group with employed doctors. [Financial Management Planner: Success Points](https://files.optometrybusiness.com/Best%20Practices%20of%20Financial%20Management.pdf).

An older textbook chapter gives a historical **25%–35% adjusted net-to-gross income range for dispensing optometry practices**. It discusses service mix, overhead, and debt as considerations when comparing practices.

Use that range within its historical valuation context. [Valuation and Purchase of an Eye Care Practice](https://files.optometrybusiness.com/BAO-Textbook-Chapter-8.pdf).

These references support a careful comparison process. They do not establish a single profit margin that every optometry business should achieve.

## Build a Comparable Margin From Your Own Books

### Define the calculation before entering figures

Write the intended calculation at the top of your worksheet:

**Margin = the selected profit measure divided by the matching revenue measure, expressed as a percentage.**

The essential work is defining those terms. If the reference uses collections, identify the collections figure in your reporting. If your statement uses another revenue basis, flag that difference before comparing the results.

Give each calculation a descriptive label. “Pre-tax net-income margin” tells a reader more than “profit.” “Doctor compensation plus EBITDA” makes the treatment of doctor pay visible. Avoid renaming an unfamiliar source metric simply to fit an existing spreadsheet column.

For every comparison, record:

- The reporting period.
- The accounting basis used in your books.
- The revenue or collections figure selected.
- The expenses included in the calculation.
- The treatment of doctor compensation.
- Any adjustments and their supporting explanation.

Keep the source definition beside your own calculation. If you cannot reconcile the terms, leave the comparison open for your bookkeeper or accountant instead of forcing an apparent match.

### Document owner compensation and adjustments

Prepare a separate schedule for owner and associate compensation. Identify where salaries, benefits, and other doctor-related amounts appear in the books. Ask your accountant to confirm how owner distributions should be treated in the particular metric you are calculating.

This matters because the published references explicitly differ in their treatment of doctor pay. A before-compensation cash-flow measure and an after-compensation earnings measure answer different questions.

Keep reported results and adjusted results in separate columns. For each proposed adjustment, document the original account, the reason for the change, and the source definition it is intended to match.

Describe an unusual expense specifically rather than applying a broad adjustment to an entire category.

Do not subtract the published medians from each other to estimate your practice’s doctor compensation. Work from your actual compensation records and the selected benchmark’s definition.

## Choose Comparisons That Fit Your Practice

### For a solo office, make the owner’s work visible

In a solo practice, start the review by describing the owner’s roles. Include clinical work, management, bookkeeping, and administrative responsibilities. Then identify how compensation for those responsibilities appears in your financial reports.

Keep this role inventory alongside the financial worksheet. If you are considering hiring help, use it to define the work you want someone else to perform. Avoid introducing an assumed replacement cost merely to make the practice look more comparable with a published reference.

Ask your accountant to distinguish the reported accounting result from any management view that estimates compensation for work currently performed by the owner. Label the assumptions clearly.

The immediate objective is an understandable comparison: what your books report, what the benchmark includes, and where those definitions differ.

### For a group, show location results and shared expenses

For a group practice, prepare both location-level and consolidated views. Show which expenses belong directly to an office and which are shared across the organization.

Document how shared costs are allocated. Examples to examine include centralized administrative support, accounting, software, and management. Use the same stated method across the locations being compared, and explain any exceptions.

Before ranking offices by margin, check whether each report includes the same categories. Ask whether doctor compensation, shared support, and owner management work receive consistent treatment.

Keep a visible reconciliation between the location reports and the consolidated statement. When an expense appears only at the group level, identify it explicitly so the location comparison remains understandable.

### Match the practice model and reporting period

Service mix also belongs in the comparison. The historical textbook reference specifically discusses dispensing practices and explains that service mix, overhead, and debt affect comparisons.

[Valuation and Purchase of an Eye Care Practice](https://files.optometrybusiness.com/BAO-Textbook-Chapter-8.pdf).

Describe your practice model before choosing a reference. Note whether the report covers the whole business or a particular service area. Do not apply a whole-practice benchmark to an optical department or clinical service line without establishing a comparable definition.

Choose matching reporting periods for your internal trend review. Add brief notes about changes such as a relocation, staffing transition, or service launch. Treat these as circumstances to investigate rather than automatic explanations for a favorable or unfavorable result.

## Turn a Margin Gap Into a Focused Review

### Check the reporting before changing spending

When your result differs from a reference, start with the calculation. Confirm that the selected accounts, reporting period, and compensation treatment match your worksheet.

Trace unclear balances back to supporting records. Look for expenses posted to an unexpected category, inconsistent treatment between periods, or adjustments that lack an explanation. Resolve those questions with the person responsible for the books.

Separate confirmed findings from open questions. A useful review note might say that an expense classification has been corrected, while a change in collections remains under investigation. This keeps the next decision tied to evidence.

Avoid assigning a cause to a margin difference simply because a particular expense looks prominent. A benchmark comparison identifies a question for review; the practice’s records must supply the explanation.

### Connect expense categories to the work being performed

After checking the reporting, examine the activity behind the expense. For administrative staffing, document responsibilities, coverage arrangements, handoffs, and unresolved tasks. For software, list the functions used, the team responsible, and any overlapping subscriptions.

For optical purchasing, assemble the relevant purchasing records and inventory reports. For occupancy, separate the commitments already in place from decisions still available to the practice.

Frame the review around specific questions:

- What work or service does this expense support?
- Who is responsible for that work?
- What records explain the change from the prior period?
- Is the expense recurring, temporary, or still unclear?
- What would need to change operationally before the spending could change?

These questions create a basis for a decision without importing an unsupported expense target. Document the answers before selecting a staffing, purchasing, or vendor action.

### Evaluate administrative support using your own records

Apply the same review to internal staffing, software, and outside [optometry practice support](/industries/optometry/). Define the work first: call handling, administrative follow-up, bookkeeping support, or another clearly described responsibility.

For any proposed service, list what is included, what your team would retain, and who would manage exceptions. Record quoted fees, implementation requirements, internal oversight, and any existing costs that would continue.

Keep projected savings out of the baseline comparison unless you can identify the expense that would actually change. Likewise, do not enter an assumed revenue increase as an established benefit.

If you proceed with a change, decide in advance which financial records and operational observations you will review afterward. Evaluate the actual result using the same definitions established before the change.

## Make Benchmark Reviews Useful Month After Month

### Maintain a concise comparison worksheet

Use a recurring worksheet that preserves the meaning of each figure. The format can be simple, provided another person can follow the calculation and understand the source.

| Worksheet field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Metric | The full name of the profit or cash-flow measure |
| Source | The publication, link, and relevant reporting period |
| Revenue basis | The exact revenue or collections figure used |
| Compensation treatment | How owner and associate doctor pay is handled |
| Reported result | The result taken from the practice’s books |
| Adjustments | Each proposed change with its explanation |
| Comparison limits | Differences in practice model, sample, or definitions |
| Follow-up | The question, responsible person, and review date |

Retain prior versions so that changes to the method remain visible. When you revise an account mapping or adjustment, note the revision before drawing a trend conclusion.

Use the published benchmark as an external reference alongside your own historical reporting. Keep both visible rather than replacing the practice’s history with a target taken from an unrelated setting.

### Finish each review with a specific decision

End the review by selecting a concrete next action. That might be clarifying an account classification, documenting doctor compensation, reviewing a vendor agreement, or assigning responsibility for an administrative task.

State what evidence supports the action and what remains uncertain. Assign responsibility and establish when the result will be reviewed. If the evidence is incomplete, the next action can be to obtain the missing information.

Bring unresolved accounting definitions to your accountant with the source passage and your worksheet attached. A focused question about a named metric is easier to work through than a general concern that the margin seems low.
