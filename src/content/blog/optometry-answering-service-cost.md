**Optometry answering service cost** depends on the work you want covered, the hours you need, and the unit the provider bills: monthly plans with included minutes, per-call or per-provider plans, or hourly virtual receptionist support. To compare providers, request a written estimate that includes the base charge, applicable usage charges, and setup terms.

For a solo owner, the immediate need might be answering calls while the front desk helps someone in the office. For a group operator, it might be directing callers to the correct location and following different provider schedules. Both owners need a clear answer to the same question: what work will this agreement cover, and what will remain with the practice?

This guide explains the pricing structures in published provider examples and offers a way to compare proposals. Use it alongside the scope of a [medical answering service](/solutions/medical-answering-service/) to connect the proposed fee with the responsibilities you actually want to delegate.

## How optometry answering service pricing works

### Monthly plans with included minutes

For a minute-based proposal, ask the provider to define billable time. Does the clock include hold time, transfers, message preparation, or follow-up work? Request those definitions in the agreement, along with the treatment of unused minutes.

Use aggregate call-duration information from your phone system when preparing the estimate. Ask the provider to distinguish measured usage from assumptions. If your practice does not yet have reliable usage information, label the estimate as provisional and request a clear explanation of how the bill would change with different usage.

### Per-call and per-provider plans

Next Call In publishes per-call medical answering plans, a daytime overflow rate billed by minute, and a per-provider option for groups. It also lists setup and base fees. These are examples of different billing arrangements within the same provider's offerings. [Next Call In pricing](https://nextcallin.com/pricing).

Ask what counts as a billable call, including repeat calls, transfers, wrong numbers, and calls that end before the request is completed. For a per-provider proposal, clarify which clinicians are included and how roster changes affect the agreement.

Keep the billing unit visible on your comparison sheet. A per-provider quote and a per-call quote need a shared coverage description before you can evaluate them together. Ask each vendor to price the same hours, call categories, and escalation responsibilities.

### Hourly virtual receptionist support

EyeCareWorks publishes hourly virtual receptionist pricing and describes work including scheduling, insurance verification, and follow-up. This is a vendor-produced description of its own service, so confirm the proposed duties and terms directly. [EyeCareWorks virtual receptionist service](https://www.eyecareworks.com/virtual-receptionist).

When evaluating an hourly proposal, specify what the receptionist should do during the covered hours. Include the order of priority between incoming calls, appointment work, and administrative follow-up. Ask how the provider documents billed time and handles absences or requests outside the agreed schedule.

The useful comparison is the proposed assignment: hours, responsibilities, access, and supervision. Avoid treating an hourly receptionist proposal as interchangeable with a message-taking plan before those details are defined.

### AI phone coverage

RingIQ publishes after-hours and per-call AI coverage options and describes scheduling and urgent-call transfer features. Treat these as a specific vendor example when comparing AI phone coverage with live answering. [RingIQ service plans](https://www.ringiq.ai).

For an AI proposal, ask the vendor to demonstrate the workflows you intend to purchase. Include an appointment request, a request outside the system's authority, and a transfer to a person. Use fictional test scenarios without patient information.

Document what happens when a request cannot be completed, who receives it, and how any subsequent handling is billed. Evaluate the service against your written requirements without assuming a particular cost or performance advantage from the technology alone.

## Define the work before requesting a quote

### Specify what a completed call means

Write a plain-language definition of completion for each call category. For an appointment request, does completion mean sending a message, offering approved appointment options, or recording an appointment in the practice system? For an insurance question, should the service route the request or perform an agreed verification task?

An MGMA poll identifies eligibility, prior authorization, and scheduling as time-consuming phone tasks for medical practices. That finding provides useful categories for a workload review, rather than a measure of your own practice's workload. [MGMA phone-work poll](https://prod.mgma.com/mgma-stat/phones-are-still-a-backlog-costing-medical-practices-time).

Create a short responsibility list with a defined stopping point for each task. Include where the service records the outcome and which practice role takes over when an exception occurs. Give vendors the same list so their proposals address the same assignment.

### Map hours, locations, and handoffs

Describe when calls should reach the service: during office hours, [when the front desk cannot answer](/solutions/front-desk-outsourcing/), after closing, or during specified closures. Ask vendors to confirm which of those arrangements their proposal includes.

For a solo office, document the main schedule, approved call destinations, and responsibility for reviewing messages. For a group, add location-specific hours, provider assignments, and the rules for requests that could involve another office.

Include exceptions such as a changed clinic schedule or an unavailable contact. Name the practice role responsible for updating instructions and ask how the provider receives those changes. A proposal should explain how your intended routing will work, including the handoff back to your team.

## Build a budget from the full proposal

### Put the terms in a common comparison sheet

Ask each provider to return an itemized proposal using your coverage description. Keep recurring charges, usage charges, and startup items distinct. For any item without a stated price or inclusion, mark it as unresolved.

The following worksheet focuses on questions to answer; it does not assume that every provider charges for every item.

| Proposal item | What to confirm |
|---|---|
| Base plan | Coverage hours, included work, and billing period |
| Usage allowance | Included calls or minutes and treatment of unused allowances |
| Additional usage | Applicable rate and the exact billable unit |
| Hourly support | Agreed schedule, task priorities, and time documentation |
| Provider coverage | Included clinicians and treatment of roster changes |
| Setup | Configuration, instructions, and any separate setup charge |
| Scheduling access | Supported workflow and whether access is included |
| Transfers and follow-up | Ownership of unfinished requests and billing treatment |
| Account changes | Terms for changing coverage, pausing, or ending service |

Retain the written answers with the proposal. If a vendor describes an item as included, ask for the corresponding scope in the agreement. This gives the owner and practice manager the same reference when reviewing the eventual invoice.

### Examine quieter and busier usage scenarios

Ask the vendor to apply its proposed terms to quieter, typical, and busier usage scenarios drawn from your own aggregate records. Keep the requested tasks and hours consistent across proposals. Avoid sending patient details as part of a pricing exercise.

For each scenario, request a breakdown of the base charge and applicable usage charges. For hourly coverage, show the proposed hours and any agreed changes in coverage. For a provider-based agreement, use the actual roster intended for that proposal.

Where information is missing, identify the assumption rather than filling the gap with an industry average. Ask when you can revisit the plan after observing actual usage and what terms apply to a change. The result should be an estimate you can trace back to the agreement.

## Compare outsourced coverage with in-house staffing

### Use wage data as a starting point

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes national receptionist wage data and describes duties that include answering calls and scheduling appointments. Its occupational description says receptionists “take messages, schedule appointments, and maintain employee files.” Wage data offers a comparison point, but wages exclude other employment costs. [BLS receptionist occupation profile](https://www.bls.gov/OOH/office-and-administrative-support/receptionists.htm).

Build your internal comparison from your own staffing records and proposed responsibilities. Include the employment costs relevant to your practice, along with the hours assigned to the work. Keep any assumptions visible.

Compare equivalent assignments wherever possible. If the employee role includes in-person reception and other office duties, identify those separately from the phone coverage under consideration. That distinction belongs in the purchasing decision even when both options sit within the front-office budget.

### Account for the work your team will retain

List the responsibilities that remain inside the practice under each proposal. These might include reviewing messages, resolving scheduling exceptions, updating provider instructions, and supervising the vendor relationship.

Do not assume that purchasing answering coverage changes your staffing budget in a particular way. Instead, document the intended operating arrangement. Will your existing team keep the same schedule? Which phone tasks will move? Who will review unresolved requests?

Treat the vendor invoice and the retained internal work as separate parts of the decision. If the objective is to support the front desk during a particular part of the day, evaluate the proposal against that assignment. Any later financial assessment should use your practice's actual spending and observed workflow.

## Include privacy arrangements and urgent-call handling

### Review business associate terms

HHS guidance explains business associates and the required elements of their contracts with covered entities. It states that the relevant “satisfactory assurances must be in writing.” Use this guidance when evaluating the privacy arrangements for an answering service that will handle protected health information. [HHS business associate guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/ocr/privacy/hipaa/understanding/coveredentities/businessassociates.pdf).

Have the appropriate practice reviewer examine the proposed business associate arrangement alongside the service agreement. Ask the provider to explain how the contracted work, access permissions, and message-handling procedures fit together.

Keep these questions attached to the actual proposal. Request written clarification of responsibilities before granting access to patient information. Evaluate the documented arrangement rather than treating a marketing description as the complete privacy review.

### Define the urgent-call escalation process

Optometric Management discusses staff phone protocols and the use of an answering service to route after-hours calls. Its guidance provides an optometry-specific reference when evaluating this part of the service scope. [Optometric Management on ocular emergency triage](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2022/october/teach-ocular-emergency-triage-to-your-staff).

Ask your clinical leadership to approve the instructions used for urgent-call routing. The proposal should identify the designated contact, the backup route, and what the service does when it cannot reach the intended recipient.

Keep clinical decisions with the practice's authorized clinical team. Ask the vendor to demonstrate the agreed handoff using fictional scenarios and explain whether the quoted plan includes that workflow. Include responsibility for maintaining the contact roster and reviewing unresolved escalations.

## Choose coverage that fits your practice

### For a solo owner, start with a defined need

A solo practice can frame the purchase around a specific coverage requirement. Write down the part of the day or category of calls you want the service to handle, along with the point at which your staff should take over.

For example, a scope can focus on overflow appointment requests during office hours. Another can focus on after-hours message handling and approved routing. These are planning options, not assumptions about what your office needs.

Ask for a proposal that matches the selected scope and explains the terms for changing it. Keep the owner or practice manager responsible for instructions, unresolved requests, and invoice review. You do not need a group-wide project to make this comparison useful.

### For group operators, document shared and local rules

For a group, separate instructions that apply everywhere from those that depend on the office or clinician. Specify the greeting, call categories, scheduling permissions, and message destinations that should be shared. Then document location-specific exceptions.

Ask how the provider will identify the intended office and who will maintain the routing information. Clarify whether usage allowances apply across the group or separately to each account, location, or provider, where relevant to the proposed plan.

Request visibility into both the overall agreement and the work assigned to each office. Keep responsibility for local follow-up explicit. The same comparison worksheet used by a solo owner can support this review, with additional detail for each location.

### Review the proposal against the original assignment

Before selecting a provider, read the quote beside your original task list. Check that each requested responsibility has an owner, each charge has a definition, and each exception has a documented handoff.

Use fictional call scenarios to examine how the proposed service handles your scheduling rules and routing instructions. Ask the provider to show how an unresolved request appears to your team. Include billing questions in this review so the operating plan and the price describe the same service.

The decision should rest on a defined assignment, understandable terms, and a workable arrangement for the responsibilities your practice retains.
