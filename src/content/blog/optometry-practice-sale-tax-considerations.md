Optometry practice sale tax considerations center on the transaction structure, purchase-price allocation, capital versus ordinary income, and when sale proceeds become taxable. Review these together with your CPA and transaction attorney before accepting terms, especially when seller financing could leave depreciation recapture reportable before all payments arrive ([IRS Publication 544](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p544?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-text-block) and [IRS Publication 537](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537)).

## Establish What Your Proposed Sale Includes

### Confirm the transaction structure

Begin with a written description of what the buyer proposes to acquire. Ask whether the agreement transfers practice assets, ownership interests, or a combination of interests and assets.

Have your attorney identify the selling parties and your CPA confirm the tax treatment of the proposed structure.

Bring your existing entity documents to that discussion. Avoid choosing a structure solely because another owner used it successfully. Instead, request a short explanation of how the proposed agreement fits your practice's ownership and tax circumstances.

For an owner with several offices, prepare an ownership map showing which entity owns each location's assets. For a solo owner, a simple asset-and-entity list serves the same purpose. Mark any proposed exclusions so the review addresses the actual transaction.

### Define the outcome you need from the sale

Write down what the transition should accomplish: retirement, a reduced clinical schedule, succession to an associate, or continued work under new ownership. Then describe your cash needs at closing and afterward without assuming that every component of an offer will be immediately available.

Use those priorities as instructions for your advisers. Request a projection that distinguishes closing proceeds, later payments, transaction expenses, debt payments, and estimated taxes. Keep assumptions visible beside the projected result.

Tax planning belongs within the broader [optometry practice transitions](/optometry-practice-transitions/) discussion, alongside ownership goals, timing, and your future role. A proposal should be evaluated against all of those priorities before you decide it fits.

## Review Purchase-Price Allocation Before Agreeing to Terms

### Understand why the assets need separate attention

The IRS explains that a business sale generally involves separate assets: “each asset is treated as being sold separately for determining the treatment of gain or loss.” Inventory, depreciable property, and goodwill therefore belong in the allocation discussion; the entire sale should not simply be assumed to produce capital gain.

[IRS Publication 544](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p544?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-text-block)

For your review, assemble an asset list that reflects what is actually included. Identify optical inventory, clinical equipment, furniture, and any goodwill addressed in the proposed agreement. Mark leased items and assets excluded from the sale so your advisers can resolve their treatment.

Ask your CPA to explain the proposed classification of each material category in plain language. The useful deliverable is a schedule connecting the assets in the agreement to the tax analysis, with unresolved classifications clearly identified.

### Negotiate allocation with the overall economics

Optometric Management identifies purchase-price allocation as a factor that can greatly affect the seller's after-tax proceeds.

Its guidance states, “This allocation needs to be negotiated.” [Ready to Buy or Sell a Practice?](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2016/november/ready-to-buy-or-sell-a-practice)

Request the buyer's proposed allocation while the commercial terms are still being discussed. Have your advisers compare it with the supporting asset records and explain any disputed amounts. Keep a written record of the assumptions behind your position.

If the allocation changes during negotiations, request an updated tax projection before accepting the revision. Use the revised projection to decide whether the overall package still meets your goals. Avoid treating an unchanged headline price as a reason to skip that review.

## Evaluate Seller Financing and Depreciation Recapture Together

### Examine the proposed payment schedule

Seller financing deserves a separate discussion about payment timing and tax treatment.

The American Optometric Association discusses its potential tax advantages in practice transitions, while IRS guidance explains how installment reporting applies to business sales and their individual assets.

[AOA: Transition Right](https://www.aoa.org/news/practice-management/perfect-your-practice/transition-right); [IRS Publication 537](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537)

Have your CPA identify which portions of the proposed transaction qualify for installment treatment. Request a payment-by-payment forecast showing the expected receipts and associated tax reporting. Keep the proposed note and asset allocation beside that forecast during the review.

Also discuss the financing terms with your attorney: the payment schedule, security, default provisions, and your options if payments stop. Evaluate whether you are comfortable remaining financially connected to the buyer after you stop owning the practice.

### Identify recapture before relying on deferred payments

Depreciation recapture generally must be reported in the year of sale, even when the seller receives payments later. The IRS expressly includes situations “whether or not an installment payment was received that year.” [IRS Publication 537](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537)

Provide your CPA with the depreciation schedules for the assets included in the sale. Ask for the recapture calculation and its effect on the sale-year projection before agreeing to the down payment and later installments.

Then compare expected closing cash with the tax funding your CPA recommends. Include a planning scenario in which a later buyer payment arrives late. This exercise should answer a practical question: what funds would you use to meet the obligations in your plan while waiting?

Treat any potential tax advantage as a conclusion to be established through that review. A payment schedule alone is not a complete tax analysis.

## Review Your Post-Sale Role Alongside the Purchase Agreement

### Keep compensation for future work visible

If you intend to continue practicing, review the employment or consulting proposal alongside the sale documents. Optometric Management specifically discusses financing and post-sale employment terms as part of the seller's transaction decisions.

[Ready to Buy or Sell a Practice?](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2016/november/ready-to-buy-or-sell-a-practice)

Prepare separate summaries of what you receive for the practice and what you expect to receive for future work. Ask your CPA to explain the treatment of each payment, and have your attorney clarify the obligations attached to it.

Describe the clinical schedule you want, the duration of your commitment, and the circumstances in which you could leave. Revisit your personal budget using those proposed terms. Your decision should reflect the work you are willing to perform after closing, as well as the sale itself.

### Examine conditional payments and retained ownership

If a proposal includes payments tied to future events or an ownership interest you retain, list each component separately for review. Record what must happen before you receive cash, who determines whether a condition has been met, and which documents govern the arrangement.

Request a specific tax analysis for those provisions. Leave an unresolved item marked as unresolved in your comparison rather than assigning it an assumed tax outcome.

For personal planning, show cash expected at closing separately from later or conditional amounts. Ask your advisers to explain the assumptions needed to include each item in your forecast. This creates a clearer basis for deciding how much uncertainty you are willing to accept.

## Prepare the Records and Reporting Plan

### Assemble a focused financial packet

Give your CPA a transaction packet organized around the proposed sale. Include the draft agreement, proposed allocation, relevant tax returns, asset and depreciation schedules, ownership documents, and any earlier acquisition records your CPA requests.

Add a list of missing records and assign someone to locate each item. For equipment, match the description in the sale schedule with the description in the accounting records. Flag unexplained differences for review rather than silently changing either document.

Keep this tax packet focused on financial and transaction information. Use aggregate practice reports where appropriate and exclude patient-level details from the planning materials.

The [records to prepare before diligence](/blog/optometry-practice-records-before-diligence/) article provides a related organizational starting point. Let your CPA define which supporting records are necessary for the tax work.

### Coordinate Form 8594 and later adjustments

The IRS instructions explain when buyers and sellers must use Form 8594 to report a business asset sale and its allocation. They also address supplemental reporting when consideration changes after the sale. [Instructions for Form 8594](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/i8594.pdf)

Before closing, assign responsibility for determining whether the form applies and preparing the seller's reporting. Ask your advisers to coordinate the allocation schedule with the buyer's team and resolve any discrepancies in the underlying documents.

Establish a process for notifying your CPA about later purchase-price changes. Keep the final agreement, allocation, closing statement, and amendments together so the preparer can evaluate them as a connected record.

Do not treat a price adjustment as a bookkeeping detail to mention only when the return is nearly finished. Send the supporting documents when the adjustment is agreed upon.

## Compare Offers Using Consistent Assumptions

### Build a practical decision worksheet

Ask your CPA to prepare comparable projections for the offers you are seriously considering. Use the same personal cash needs and planning assumptions unless a proposal specifically changes them.

Label estimates and unresolved items so a preliminary projection is not mistaken for a final calculation.

A worksheet can organize the discussion without substituting for professional tax calculations:

| Decision area | Information to request | Owner's decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transaction structure | Written description and CPA analysis | Whether the proposed structure fits your situation |
| Asset allocation | Proposed schedule and supporting records | Whether to accept or negotiate the allocation |
| Closing proceeds | Projection of receipts, payments, expenses, and taxes | Whether available cash supports your plans |
| Seller financing | Payment schedule, tax forecast, and legal terms | Whether the timing and obligations are acceptable |
| Future work | Employment or consulting terms | Whether the commitment matches your transition goals |
| Conditional proceeds | Payment conditions and unresolved tax questions | How to account for uncertainty in personal planning |

Use the worksheet to identify the next decision, not merely to rank offers by the largest stated price.

### Resolve questions that require individual analysis

Have your CPA identify any state or local tax questions that need separate review. If you own the office property, ask whether a proposed property sale or lease belongs in a separate analysis. If several owners are selling, request clarity about which calculations apply to each owner.

Avoid filling these gaps with another practice's experience or a general online example. Provide the documents your advisers need and ask for a written explanation of the assumptions used.

Before accepting final terms, hold a joint review with your CPA and attorney. Work through open questions, identify which answers require a contract change, and decide who will obtain each missing item. Keep the discussion tied to the documents you intend to sign.

## Carry the Tax Plan Through Closing and Beyond

### Reconcile the final documents

Review the final purchase agreement, allocation schedule, financing documents, and closing statement against the version used for your tax projection. If a material term has changed, have the relevant adviser update the analysis before you rely on it.

Pay particular attention to revised payment dates, changed asset descriptions, and adjustments to the amount payable at closing. Ask for an explanation of any difference between the projected receipts and the closing statement.

Keep a dated copy of the completed projection with its assumptions. The purpose is to preserve a clear record of what was reviewed and which version of the transaction it describes.

### Assign responsibilities after the sale

Before closing, agree on who will provide the final documents to your tax preparer, track later buyer payments, and report amendments or adjustments. Request a calendar of the tax actions your CPA identifies for your transaction, using the rules and forms applicable to your actual closing year.

If you continue working in the practice, keep the records for that arrangement organized separately from the sale records for review. Revisit your personal cash plan when the final closing figures and your adviser's tax estimates are available.
