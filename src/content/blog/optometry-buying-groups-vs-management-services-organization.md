## How Do Buying Groups and MSOs Differ for Multi-Location Optometry?

The fundamental distinction comes down to scope and operational depth. Buying groups originated as purchasing cooperatives, allowing independent practices to pool volume for vendor discounts on frames, lenses, contact lenses, and equipment. MSOs take a fundamentally different approach by providing comprehensive non-clinical administrative services including billing, credentialing, staffing, facilities management, and technology infrastructure.

For operations leaders managing multiple locations, this distinction matters because it determines what problems each model actually solves.

## What Operational Challenges Do Multi-Location Groups Face?

Before evaluating affiliation options, operations leaders must identify where their current pain points actually exist. Based on industry patterns, multi-location optometry groups consistently struggle with several core challenges.

### Disparate Systems and Data Silos

Groups that grow through acquisition often inherit different EHR and practice management systems at each location. A patient who visits Location A cannot easily reschedule at Location B because their records exist in separate databases. This fragmentation creates friction for patients and administrative overhead for staff who must navigate multiple platforms.

### Inconsistent Patient Access and Call Handling

Industry data indicates that multi-location practices miss up to 35% of calls during peak hours when front desk staff juggle phone duties with in-office patients. Without centralized overflow protocols, each location operates as an island. Patients calling a busy location cannot be seamlessly routed to another site with available staff. This operational gap directly impacts [patient access across growing networks](/blog/optometry-network-operations-scale/).

### Staff Turnover and Training Complexity

Healthcare front office positions experience approximately 45% annual turnover. For a 5-location group, this means constantly recruiting, onboarding, and training new staff while attempting to maintain consistent patient experience standards. Each location develops its own informal procedures, making it difficult to establish standardized protocols.

### Billing and Revenue Cycle Fragmentation

Credentialing, claims submission, and denial management become exponentially complex across locations. Multi-location groups frequently discover that billing practices vary significantly between sites, leading to inconsistent reimbursement rates and revenue leakage.

These challenges intensify as location count increases. The operational complexity of managing [healthcare operations post-acquisition](/blog/healthcare-operations-ma-integration/) requires systematic solutions rather than location-by-location fixes.

## How Does Each Model Handle Patient Access and Front Office Operations?

This is where the practical differences between buying groups and MSOs become most apparent for multi-location operators.

### Buying Groups: Limited Operational Support

Traditional buying groups focus on product procurement rather than operational infrastructure. While some have evolved to include educational resources, networking opportunities, and basic consulting, they typically do not provide:

- Centralized call handling or overflow routing
- Unified EHR integration across locations
- Staffing solutions for front desk positions
- Quality assurance for patient interactions
- Real-time analytics across multiple sites

For groups where supply costs represent the primary concern, buying group membership delivers measurable ROI through vendor discounts. However, operations leaders dealing with missed calls, inconsistent patient experience, or staffing shortages will find limited solutions through traditional buying group membership.

### MSOs: Comprehensive Administrative Infrastructure

MSOs approach multi-location operations differently by providing the administrative backbone that enables scale. A well-structured MSO affiliation can deliver:

**Centralized Patient Access:** Unified phone systems with intelligent routing ensure patients reach a live representative regardless of which location they call. Overflow protocols route calls to available staff across the network rather than sending patients to voicemail.

**Technology Integration:** MSOs invest in connecting disparate practice management systems, enabling patient records to flow across locations. This integration allows patients to schedule at any convenient location without administrative friction.

**Standardized Protocols:** Rather than allowing each location to develop independent procedures, MSOs implement consistent workflows for scheduling, intake, billing, and patient communication.

**Performance Analytics:** Centralized reporting provides visibility into call answer rates, appointment conversion, no-show percentages, and revenue metrics across all locations through a single dashboard.

Understanding [how MSO affiliation works](/blog/joining-an-optometric-management-service-organization/) helps operations leaders evaluate whether this level of support aligns with their scaling requirements.

## Which Model Delivers Better ROI for Growing Groups?

ROI calculations differ significantly based on where your group's operational costs and revenue leakage actually occur.

### Buying Group ROI: Supply Cost Reduction

Buying groups deliver clear, measurable returns on product procurement. A group purchasing $500,000 annually in frames, lenses, and equipment might achieve 15-25% savings through collective purchasing power. At $75,000-$125,000 in annual savings against membership dues of $2,000-$5,000 per year, the ROI calculation is straightforward.

However, this analysis only captures one cost category. For groups struggling with patient access challenges, the missed call revenue impact often dwarfs supply cost savings.

### MSO ROI: Operational Efficiency and Revenue Recovery

MSO ROI calculations are more complex because they address multiple cost and revenue factors simultaneously.

## What Questions Should Operations Leaders Ask Before Choosing?

The decision between buying group membership and MSO affiliation requires honest assessment of your group's current state and growth objectives. Consider these evaluation questions:

### Questions About Current Operations

1. What percentage of incoming calls currently go to voicemail during peak hours?
2. Can patients schedule appointments at any of your locations through a single system?
3. How long does it take to onboard a new front desk employee to full productivity?
4. Do you have visibility into call answer rates and appointment conversion across all locations?
5. How much time do your optometrists spend on administrative tasks versus patient care?

### Questions About Growth Plans

1. How many locations do you plan to add in the next 24-36 months?
2. Are you considering private equity partnership or preparing for a future transaction?
3. Do your expansion plans include acquiring existing practices with different systems?
4. What is your target staffing model as you scale?

### Questions for Potential Partners

When evaluating either buying groups or MSOs, request specific information about multi-location capabilities:

- What EHR and practice management systems do you support?
- How do you handle patient access across multiple sites?
- What reporting and analytics capabilities do you provide?
- How are performance metrics tracked and shared?
- What is your implementation timeline for new locations?

These questions help determine whether a prospective partner can actually address your operational challenges or simply offers a different version of the status quo.

## The Hybrid Approach: Combining Both Models

Many multi-location groups discover that buying groups and MSOs address different operational needs. Rather than viewing these as mutually exclusive options, successful enterprise operators often combine both approaches.

A typical hybrid structure includes:

**Buying Group Membership** for vendor discounts on frames, lenses, instruments, and optical supplies. The purchasing power of collective membership delivers ROI regardless of other operational decisions.

**MSO Affiliation** for administrative infrastructure, technology integration, and patient access management. The MSO handles back-office operations while optometrists retain clinical control.

**Specialized Service Partners** for specific functions that neither buying groups nor general MSOs address optimally. This might include dedicated patient recall programs, specialized billing services, or [centralized patient access solutions](/blog/multi-location-healthcare-intake-guide/).

The hybrid approach allows groups to capture cost savings on supplies while building operational infrastructure for scale. This model has become increasingly common as both buying groups and MSOs evolve their service offerings.

Industry observers note that buying groups have begun rebranding as "member services organizations" and adding consulting, education, and technology resources. Meanwhile, MSOs have expanded beyond traditional administrative services to include supply procurement and vendor management. The boundaries between these categories continue to blur.

## How to Evaluate Affiliation Partners as an Enterprise

For operations leaders at groups managing 3+ locations, the evaluation process should focus on enterprise-grade capabilities rather than features designed for single practices.

### Technology Integration Requirements

Any affiliation partner must demonstrate ability to:

- Connect with your existing EHR systems or provide a migration path
- Deliver centralized reporting across all locations
- Support unified patient records accessible from any site
- Integrate with your billing and revenue cycle systems

[EHR and PMS integration capabilities](/blog/ehr-pms-integration-centralized-scheduling-enterprise/) vary significantly between partners. Request specific references from groups with similar technology environments.

### Scalability Assessment

Evaluate how the partner handles growth:

- What is the onboarding process for new locations?
- How quickly can new sites be integrated into centralized systems?
- What resources are available during acquisition integration?
- Does the partner have experience with your target geographic markets?

### Financial Transparency

Understand the complete cost structure:

- What are all fees, dues, and service charges?
- How are savings or efficiencies measured and reported?
- What is the contractual commitment term?
- What are the exit provisions if the partnership does not meet expectations?

### Cultural Alignment

The operational partnership will influence how your staff and patients experience your practices:

- Does the partner's approach to patient experience match your standards?
- How do they handle quality assurance and performance management?
- What is their communication cadence with practice leadership?
- How do they balance standardization with location-specific needs?

For groups considering their practice valuation trajectory, affiliation decisions directly impact operational metrics that drive multiple expansion.

## Making the Decision for Your Multi-Location Group

The optometry buying groups vs MSO decision ultimately depends on your specific operational challenges and growth objectives.

**Choose buying group membership when:**
- Supply costs represent your primary margin concern
- Your existing patient access and front office operations perform well
- You have internal resources to manage multi-location complexity
- You prioritize minimal operational integration with external partners

**Choose MSO affiliation when:**
- Administrative burden prevents optometrists from focusing on patient care
- Patient access and call handling inconsistencies exist across locations
- You need capital and operational support for rapid expansion
- You are preparing for private equity partnership or future transaction

**Choose a hybrid approach when:**
- You want supply cost savings AND operational infrastructure
- Different challenges require different solution partners
- You are building toward enterprise-scale operations incrementally

Neither buying groups nor MSOs solve every operational challenge facing multi-location optometry groups. The most successful enterprise operators evaluate partners based on specific capabilities rather than general categories. They build operational infrastructure that enables growth rather than creating dependencies that constrain future decisions.

The eye care industry continues consolidating, with nearly 50 PE-backed platforms competing for partnership opportunities and over 13,000 practices representing potential targets. Groups that build efficient, scalable operations position themselves favorably regardless of whether they pursue private equity partnership, remain independent, or become acquirers themselves.

Your decision framework should start with clear understanding of where operational friction currently exists, what capabilities would address those challenges, and which partners can actually deliver those capabilities at the scale your group requires.
