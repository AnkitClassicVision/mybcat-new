Choosing an AI receptionist for optometry practice operations starts with deciding what should happen when someone calls. Which questions should receive an immediate answer? Which appointments may the system book? When should a staff member take over?

Those questions apply whether you own a single office or oversee a group. A solo owner can begin with a narrowly defined task, such as answering approved questions during overflow periods. A group operator can use the same approach while adding location-specific scheduling and routing instructions.

The American Optometric Association identifies appointments, services, and fees as common reasons patients call, and explains how telephone interactions influence their impression of a practice. That makes the conversation itself an essential part of evaluation. [AOA: Patient Communication](https://www.aoa.org/practice/new-doctors-of-optometry/patient-communication)

A practical starting point is to define the administrative work, demonstrate the system’s behavior, and assign responsibility for everything that needs human follow-through. This fits within the broader decisions involved in organizing [front-office support for optometry practices](/industries/optometry/).

## Define the AI receptionist’s job before choosing a vendor

### Start with specific administrative tasks

Write a job description for the proposed system. Include the questions it may answer, the actions it may take, and the circumstances that require a handoff. Make each responsibility concrete enough to demonstrate.

For example, “answer scheduling questions” leaves room for interpretation. A clearer instruction is to explain approved appointment categories, offer eligible openings, and route requests outside those categories to staff.

AI phone scheduling appears in optometry product descriptions already. AOAExcel, the AOA’s for-profit subsidiary, describes Barti EHR’s AI tool for scheduling appointments by phone. The same announcement discloses AOAExcel’s investment in and endorsement of the product. [AOAExcel: Barti EHR announcement](https://www.aoa.org/practice/aoaexcel/aoaexcel-resource-updates/aoaexcel-news/aoaexcel-endorses-its-first-ever-ehr-barti-ehr)

Use that as a concrete product example when developing evaluation questions. Ask each vendor to demonstrate the exact tasks you intend to authorize in your own workflow.

### Establish boundaries for different call types

The AOA’s paraoptometric certification outline includes telephone triage, call documentation, scheduling, reminders, and insurance questions among front-office responsibilities. It offers a useful starting checklist for mapping reception work. [AOA: Expanded CPOT Outline](https://www.aoa.org/AOA/Documents/Education/Paraoptometric/Certification/Expanded%20CPOT%20Outline%202023%20Final.pdf)

Turn that checklist into proposed operating boundaries:

| Call type | Proposed automated role | Human involvement to define |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Office hours and directions | Provide approved information for the correct office | Resolve exceptions or conflicting information |
| Routine appointment requests | Offer appointments within approved scheduling rules | Handle requests outside those rules |
| Insurance questions | Share approved general information | Review individual coverage and benefit questions |
| Symptoms or requests for clinical advice | Follow the practice’s clinician-approved routing instructions | Assign clinical assessment to the appropriate professional |
| Complaints or disputed charges | Recognize the request and initiate a handoff | Assign a staff member to resolve the concern |

These are proposed boundaries to review with your team. Avoid giving the system permission to improvise when an answer is missing. Define the next action for uncertainty, including who receives the request and what the caller should hear.

## Make scheduling rules explicit

### Describe what the system may book

Prepare an approved scheduling guide before connecting a receptionist to the appointment calendar. Specify appointment categories, eligible providers, office locations, and restrictions on changing existing appointments.

Include exceptions that staff currently manage through experience. Decide whether the system may offer another provider, suggest another office, reschedule a visit, or place a request into a staff review queue. Give each exception an explicit instruction.

For a solo practice, this might be a short guide reviewed by the owner and front-desk lead. For a group, establish common rules and record approved differences by location. Assign someone to maintain those differences when providers, services, or office hours change.

Keep insurance language equally deliberate. Approve the wording used for general participation questions, and define which questions must go to staff. The receptionist should communicate only the information and decisions you have authorized.

### Demonstrate the full calendar workflow

Ask the vendor to show what happens from the caller’s request through the resulting entry in your practice system. Clarify whether the product creates a confirmed appointment, submits a request for approval, or sends a message for staff to process.

Then examine the exception paths. What happens if the calendar connection is unavailable? What happens if an offered opening becomes unavailable before the caller accepts it? How does the system handle a rescheduling request when it cannot confirm the existing appointment?

Require the closing language to match the actual result. A pending request should be described as pending. A confirmed appointment should reflect a successful entry in the designated scheduling system.

Also specify who checks unresolved requests and how staff acknowledge completion. Include those responsibilities in the demonstration so that your evaluation covers both the conversation and the work left afterward.

## Build human support into the call flow

### Give clinical questions an approved route

Have the practice’s clinical lead define how symptom-related calls and requests for medical advice should be handled. Document the approved routing instructions, the receiving role, and the fallback when that person is unavailable.

Evaluate this behavior with synthetic scenarios that contain no real patient information. Ask the vendor to demonstrate a caller mentioning a clinical concern during an otherwise administrative conversation. Check whether the system follows the approved handoff instructions without continuing through routine booking questions.

Use the same approach for after-hours handling. Have the clinical lead approve the wording and destination for those calls. Describe the service to callers according to the coverage you have actually arranged.

Keep clinical decisions with the professionals your practice designates. The receptionist’s instructions should make its administrative role and escalation responsibilities explicit.

### Make requesting a person straightforward

Include an easy way to ask for staff assistance. Test direct requests such as “I would like to speak with someone,” along with less explicit signals that the conversation is not working.

Decide what happens when a transfer cannot be completed. Define the message the caller hears, the queue that receives the request, and the person responsible for following up. Avoid promising a callback time that your team has not agreed to support.

Set expectations for the information passed to the receiving staff member. Request only what the authorized workflow needs, and use practice-approved systems for any patient information.

Review the greeting as well. Consider clearly identifying the automated assistant and explaining how to reach a person. Evaluate pacing, interruptions, requests to repeat information, and language support with the same care as scheduling actions.

## Review privacy and outbound communication separately

### Examine the vendor’s role and data handling

HHS explains when organizations handling protected health information on a provider’s behalf may be business associates and describes business associate agreement requirements. Its examples include a third-party patient-portal chatbot providing services such as appointment scheduling. [HHS: Business Associates](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html)

HHS says a business associate agreement must describe the “permitted and required uses and disclosures of PHI.” Use that language to guide a review of the proposed service and contract. [HHS: Business Associates](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html)

Ask the vendor to document:

- What information the service receives and creates.
- Whether it retains recordings, transcripts, or call summaries.
- Who can access that information.
- Which other providers process or store it.
- How retention, deletion, and access changes are handled.
- Whether information is used for model training or other purposes.

Have the person responsible for privacy review the applicable agreements and proposed workflow before introducing patient information. Use synthetic information in initial demonstrations. Treat vendor marketing language as a prompt for questions, and obtain written answers about the actual service configuration.

### Assess outbound calls and texts as their own project

An inbound reception evaluation should specify whether outbound features are included. Ask about reminders, follow-up calls, and text messages individually, including how each function is activated.

The AOA discusses Telephone Consumer Protection Act considerations for automated calls and texts, including the healthcare messaging exemption. This is relevant when a proposed AI receptionist also contacts patients. [AOA: Using automated services to reach patients? Know the rules](https://www.aoa.org/news/practice-management/perfect-your-practice/the-wrong-patient-communication-plan-could-be-costly)

Before enabling outbound functions, have the proposed message types and communication permissions reviewed by the appropriate adviser. Ask the vendor to demonstrate how communication preferences and opt-outs are managed.

Document who approves message content and who can launch outreach. Review any expansion in purpose separately, including a change from answering incoming calls to initiating a campaign. Keep the authorized scope visible to both the vendor and your staff.

## Evaluate the service with realistic demonstrations

### Bring your own scenarios

Prepare demonstrations around the decisions your team wants to evaluate. Use synthetic scenarios without patient names, contact details, insurance identifiers, or other private information.

Useful scenarios include an ordinary appointment request, a caller changing their mind, a request for an unavailable provider, and a question missing from the approved information. Include a request for a person and a simulated interruption to the scheduling connection.

For each scenario, define the expected response before the demonstration. Record whether the system followed the approved rules, what action appeared in the connected system, and what remained for staff.

Ask to repeat scenarios after configuration changes. Have front-desk staff participate so they can assess whether the resulting task or handoff contains what they need. For a group, include representatives from offices with different scheduling arrangements.

### Review the full operating commitment

Request an itemized proposal that explains how the vendor charges for setup, usage, integrations, support, and changes. Ask which items are included in the quoted service and which require separate approval.

Include your practice’s responsibilities in the review. Who supplies and updates approved answers? Who checks unresolved requests? Who trains staff on the receiving workflow? Who contacts the vendor when a rule needs to change?

Clarify contract terms, access to configuration materials, and the process for ending the service. Ask what happens to stored information and how call routing returns to the practice’s preferred arrangement.

Evaluate the proposed service against your actual reception requirements. Keep any financial comparison grounded in your own costs and observed workload. Leave savings estimates out of the decision until you have evidence from your practice to support them.

## Introduce the workflow in a manageable scope

### Choose a starting point your team can supervise

Select an initial use case with clear boundaries. That could be approved general questions, a defined overflow period, or a limited set of appointment requests. Choose the scope according to the supervision and human coverage available.

For a solo owner, assign the office manager or another designated staff member to review unresolved work. Agree on when the owner needs to make decisions and which updates staff can approve independently.

For a group operator, document the participating offices, their exceptions, and the people authorized to change shared rules. Make location selection part of testing before introducing additional offices.

Create a fallback procedure that staff can use when the workflow is unavailable or needs correction. Identify who can pause automated handling, where incoming calls should go, and how pending tasks will be reconciled.

### Review completed work and unresolved requests

Define evaluation criteria before the pilot begins. Review whether calls reach the intended destination, appointment entries follow approved rules, and requests requiring human attention arrive with a clear owner.

Include caller experience in that review. Look for repeated requests for clarification, difficulty reaching a person, and closing language that does not match the actual outcome. Use approved systems and access controls for any review involving patient information.

Track staff work as well. Record the types of corrections and follow-up tasks the workflow creates. Discuss which problems call for a wording change, a scheduling rule change, vendor support, or a narrower scope.

Make expansion a deliberate decision. Agree on the evidence your practice needs before adding responsibilities, and review unresolved issues before authorizing the next use case.

## Choose the arrangement your practice can maintain

### Match automation to the work you want to delegate

[Compare proposed reception arrangements](/compare/vs-ai-receptionists/) by assigning each task to an owner. Identify [what the automated system will handle](/blog/ai-receptionist-vs-virtual-front-desk-answering-service/), what office staff will handle, and what any outside support team will handle.

For a solo practice, keep that responsibility map simple enough to use during a busy day. A brief guide should show where calls go and who handles unfinished requests.

For a group, preserve the same clarity across shared teams and individual locations. Name the person responsible when a request crosses those boundaries.

Use the map to evaluate the service itself. If a task has no clear owner, resolve that gap before launch. If an exception requires discretion, decide who has authority to make the decision and how the request reaches them.

### Keep information and accountability current

Assign an owner to the approved information used by the receptionist. Establish how changes to hours, providers, services, and scheduling policies are submitted, reviewed, and tested.

Ask the vendor how your team can inspect the current configuration and identify what changed. Include staff feedback in ongoing reviews, particularly when the same request repeatedly needs clarification.

The decision should rest on a workflow you can explain: what the receptionist is authorized to do, how its actions are checked, and who takes responsibility when a caller needs more help.
