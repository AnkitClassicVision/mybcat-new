A reactivation campaign can look simple from the board deck: identify overdue patients, send outreach, book the ones who respond, and recover demand that already belongs to the group.

Operationally, the risk is different. If a DSO or multi-location healthcare group launches outreach before it understands appointment capacity, the campaign can flood the wrong offices, frustrate front-desk teams, create long callback queues, and push interested patients into appointment windows that do not match provider availability.

That is why multi-location recall slot capacity planning should happen before the blast, not after demand appears.

The goal is not to predict the future perfectly. It is to make the campaign schedulable.

Leadership needs to know which locations can absorb demand, which providers have constrained availability, which appointment types should be prioritized, and how quickly outreach should ramp.

Operations needs rules that the call center, local teams, and scheduling managers can follow without improvising office by office.

For DSOs, MSOs, optometry groups, dental groups, and other healthcare operators with 3+ locations, recall capacity planning is the bridge between patient growth strategy and daily schedule reality.

## Start With Appointment Supply, Not the Outreach List

### Define the slots the campaign is allowed to use

The first mistake in a group reactivation blast is treating the schedule as if every open appointment is available for recall. It rarely is.

Some slots are reserved for urgent care, treatment continuation, provider preferences, new-patient demand, post-op visits, specialty services, or local office priorities.

A recall campaign should not consume those slots unless leadership has made an intentional tradeoff.

Before outreach begins, define the appointment inventory that is eligible for recall demand.

That may include preventive visits, hygiene recare, routine exams, annual eye exams, or other scheduled follow-up categories.

The exact appointment types will depend on the group’s specialty and operating model.

AHRQ explains that reminder and recall systems can reduce no-shows and support more preventive visits, which is directly relevant when leaders are matching recall demand to actual appointment supply.

That connection matters: recall is not just a marketing activity. It is a scheduling operation tied to access capacity.

### Separate visible availability from usable capacity

Open appointment slots are not automatically usable capacity.

A slot may appear open in the practice management system but still be operationally fragile.

It may sit with a provider who is already overloaded, in a location that has limited front-desk coverage, or in a daypart where call volume and check-in pressure are already high.

For multi-location groups, capacity planning should distinguish between:

- Slots that are open and appropriate for recall
- Slots that are open but should be protected
- Slots that require local approval
- Slots that can be released only if demand is low elsewhere
- Slots that are technically open but operationally unrealistic

MGMA’s scheduling guidance emphasizes the need for operational governance and technology as scheduling complexity grows across providers and workflows.

That is the right lens for recall planning. The work is not simply finding empty boxes on a calendar.

It is deciding which appointment inventory can be safely converted into campaign demand.

## Segment Recall Demand Before You Release It

### Group patients by scheduling complexity

A recall list should not be treated as one uniform population. Some patients are easy to schedule.

Others require insurance verification, provider matching, treatment history review, language support, clinical triage, or a specific location.

Capacity planning improves when the outreach list is segmented before launch. A simple operational segmentation might separate patients by:

- Appointment type
- Preferred or assigned location
- Provider requirement
- Time since last visit
- Insurance or eligibility workflow
- Language preference
- Prior cancellation or no-show pattern
- Need for staff review before booking

The purpose is not to create a complicated model for its own sake. The purpose is to keep high-friction demand from landing in the same queue as straightforward bookings.

Optometric Management describes recall workflows that use EHR triggers, automated text or email outreach, recall reports, staff follow-up, and labor cost analysis.

That kind of workflow thinking is useful because it treats recall as an operating system, not a one-message campaign.

### Match outreach pace to each segment

Once the list is segmented, the group can pace outreach by capacity.

Straightforward recall segments can often move faster because scheduling rules are clear.

More complex segments may need a smaller release, a dedicated queue, or staff review before live outreach.

This is especially important when a DSO is reactivating dormant patients across many locations.

A large list can create the illusion of opportunity while hiding bottlenecks inside specific offices, providers, or appointment types.

For example, a group may have meaningful availability in one region but limited availability in another.

It may have open preventive care slots but constrained treatment slots.

It may have enough total appointment inventory but not enough trained scheduling coverage to process the responses quickly.

Recall capacity planning should make those differences visible before the campaign starts.

## Build a Location-by-Location Capacity View

### Compare supply across offices

Central leadership needs a location-level view before approving a groupwide reactivation blast.

The view does not need to be fancy, but it should answer practical questions:

- Which offices can absorb recall appointments soon?
- Which locations should receive slower outreach?
- Which appointment types have the most usable capacity?
- Which providers have constraints that should be respected?
- Which offices need local confirmation before slots are released?
- Which regions should be excluded from the first campaign wave?

MGMA’s discussion of central scheduling addresses open appointment slots, supply constraints, excess capacity, and flexing scheduling operations across offices.

Those are the same dynamics that make multi-location recall slot capacity planning valuable.

A centralized team can only help if it sees where capacity exists and where it does not.

### Avoid averaging away local constraints

Enterprise operators often look at total open capacity and assume the group can handle demand. That can be misleading.

If one location has ample availability and another has almost none, the average may look comfortable while the campaign still overwhelms the constrained site.

If a particular provider is booked far out, a recall blast tied to that provider can generate demand that cannot be scheduled promptly.

If one office has staffing instability, additional campaign response can create operational drag even when the calendar appears open.

A location-by-location view protects the group from false confidence.

For DSOs and multi-location healthcare groups, the capacity plan should show both aggregate opportunity and local constraint.

Executives need the rollup. Operators need the detail.

## Decide How Central Scheduling and Local Teams Will Share Control

### Set the booking rules before launch

Recall campaigns move faster when the scheduling team knows what it is allowed to do.

Before outreach begins, define booking rules for each appointment type and location. The rules should answer:

- Which appointment types can the centralized team schedule directly?
- Which appointment types require local office review?
- Which providers are included or excluded?
- Which slots are protected?
- Which days or dayparts should be avoided?
- Which patient segments require verification before booking?
- How should overflow demand be routed?

Without these rules, the campaign creates avoidable friction. Central teams pause to ask questions.

Local teams receive inconsistent handoffs. Patients wait while staff clarify scheduling authority.

The ADA’s recare guidance describes the role of pre-appointing, monthly overdue-patient reports, and systematic outreach to keep patients active in preventive care.

That kind of system depends on repeatable operating rules. At scale, repeatability matters more than heroic manual effort.

### Define escalation paths for exceptions

No capacity plan eliminates exceptions. Some patients will need special handling. Some providers will change availability.

Some locations will ask to pause outreach. Some appointment types will fill faster than expected.

The question is whether the exceptions have a defined path.

For multi-location groups, escalation rules should be explicit.

The scheduling team should know when to escalate to a location manager, when to route to a regional operator, and when to hold a patient for review.

The goal is to prevent small scheduling uncertainties from becoming abandoned leads or frustrated patient experiences.

Centralized scheduling works best when local nuance is captured in rules, not trapped in individual memory.

## Use Metrics That Reveal Schedule Health

### Track recall capacity, not just outreach volume

A recall campaign can look productive while still creating schedule problems.

If the team only tracks messages sent, calls made, or patients reached, leadership may miss whether the campaign is consuming the right capacity.

The more useful view connects outreach activity to appointment availability, booking outcomes, cancellation behavior, and schedule gaps.

The ADA identifies practice metrics tied to recall-slot planning, including hygiene patients scheduled, cancellation and no-show rates, unproductive schedule gaps, and reporting from practice-management software.

Those metrics point operators toward the schedule health questions that matter after a recall campaign begins.

A practical dashboard should show whether the campaign is helping fill the right appointment inventory or simply shifting pressure from one part of the system to another.

### Watch cancellation and rescheduling signals

Recall outreach can also surface cancellations and rescheduling opportunities.

A peer-reviewed systematic review found that appointment reminders improve attendance and can increase cancellations or rescheduling, which can help practices reallocate slots more efficiently.

For operators, this is important because recall and reminder workflows interact with the same appointment inventory.

If patients cancel earlier, the team may be able to refill capacity. If rescheduling demand increases, the call center needs enough coverage and clear rules.

If the cancellation pattern is concentrated in certain locations, leadership may need to adjust outreach pacing or schedule templates.

The point is not to chase one metric in isolation. It is to understand how recall demand, reminders, cancellations, and available slots move together.

## Pace the Campaign in Waves

### Launch with a controlled release

The safest reactivation campaigns rarely begin with the full list.

A controlled release gives the team a chance to test assumptions.

Are the scheduling rules clear? Are patients choosing the expected locations? Are certain appointment types filling faster than planned? Are local teams receiving clean handoffs? Is the centralized team seeing edge cases that were not considered?

A wave-based launch lets operators adjust before the campaign reaches full scale.

That matters in any multi-location environment, but especially in groups where appointment supply varies by office.

A small launch wave can reveal whether the capacity model is realistic. If the campaign produces more complex scheduling demand than expected, leadership can slow down.

If the selected locations absorb demand smoothly, the next wave can expand.

### Use stop conditions, not just goals

Campaign plans often define goals. They should also define stop conditions.

Stop conditions are the signals that tell the team to pause, slow, or redirect outreach.

Examples include locations nearing appointment constraints, unresolved scheduling queues, excessive exception volume, local staffing disruptions, or a mismatch between patient demand and available appointment types.

Stop conditions protect patient experience and staff trust. They also give executives a clear way to govern the campaign without waiting for a postmortem.

A recall blast should feel controlled. If the only control mechanism is “send fewer messages next time,” the plan is too late.

## Align Recall Capacity With Patient Experience

### Make scheduling easy for the patient

Recall capacity planning is not only about protecting the operator. It is also about making the patient journey feel simple.

If a patient responds to recall outreach and cannot find a reasonable appointment, the campaign creates disappointment instead of access.

If the patient is transferred between teams, asked to repeat information, or told to call a local office after responding to centralized outreach, the workflow feels fragmented.

AOA notes that recall strategies help patients plan eye care, regulate office flow, and fill the appointment book.

That is the balance operators should aim for: a patient communication system that supports both access and operational flow.

The best capacity plans make the next step obvious. The patient responds. The team sees the correct appointment options.

The slot is booked according to approved rules. Exceptions are routed without making the patient manage the group’s internal complexity.

### Protect local teams from avoidable chaos

Local office teams are often the first to feel a poorly planned campaign.

If central outreach fills the wrong slots, local staff must clean up the schedule.

If patients are booked into appointment types that do not match provider expectations, the office absorbs the friction.

If outreach creates more callbacks than the team can handle, the local experience gets worse even when the campaign was meant to help.

Capacity planning should include local input before launch. Office managers and regional operators can identify constraints that are not obvious in the schedule template.

They may know which providers need tighter booking rules, which days are already operationally heavy, and which appointment types should be held for clinical reasons.

The goal is not to let every location design its own campaign. The goal is to centralize the work while respecting the operating realities that make each location schedulable.

## Turn Capacity Planning Into an Operating Rhythm

### Review campaign performance while it is live

A recall campaign should not be judged only after it ends.

During the campaign, operators should review schedule fill, backlog, appointment-type mix, cancellation and rescheduling behavior, local feedback, and unresolved exceptions.

This does not require a huge reporting program. It requires a consistent operating rhythm.

For multi-location groups, that rhythm might include short reviews with centralized scheduling, regional operations, and local leadership.

The conversation should focus on whether the campaign is still aligned with capacity.

If certain locations are filling too quickly, shift outreach elsewhere. If a patient segment is creating too many exceptions, adjust the rules.

If slots are opening because patients reschedule, decide whether those slots should be offered to recall patients.

The capacity plan is not a static spreadsheet. It is a control system.

### Standardize what worked

After the campaign, the group should convert lessons into standard operating procedures.

Which scheduling rules worked? Which segments were easiest to book? Which locations absorbed demand smoothly? Which exception types slowed the team down? Which reports gave leadership the clearest view of capacity?

Those answers should shape the next campaign.

Over time, the group can build a repeatable recall playbook that includes segmentation, slot governance, outreach pacing, escalation rules, reporting, and post-campaign review.

That is how recall becomes an enterprise capability rather than a one-time push.

## Know When to Add Centralized Support

### Signs the campaign needs more operating infrastructure

A DSO or healthcare group may be ready for more structured recall support when campaign complexity starts to exceed local capacity.

Common signs include inconsistent location-level follow-up, unclear ownership of overdue-patient reports, scheduling queues that build after outreach, appointment inventory that varies widely by office, and local teams that are too busy to manage systematic recall while also handling daily patient access.

This is where centralized patient access, recall support, and scheduling governance can help.

The work is not only making outbound contact. It is coordinating patient demand with usable appointment capacity across the group.

For enterprise teams, the real question is not “Can we send recall messages?” It is “Can we schedule the demand we create?”
