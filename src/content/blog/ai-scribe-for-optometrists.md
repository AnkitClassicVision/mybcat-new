An **AI scribe for optometrists** uses information from a clinical encounter to generate draft documentation. In an ambient workflow, the clinician activates a tool that captures the encounter conversation and uses it to prepare a note.

If charting follows you home, an AI scribe deserves a careful look. Whether you own a solo practice or oversee a group, the practical question is the same: how would it change the work required to produce a complete, reviewed clinical record?

Optometry Australia’s education listing describes clinical note generation alongside the practical considerations of workflow integration, privacy, and clinical responsibility.

Those considerations belong in the buying decision from the beginning.

[Optometry Australia education listing](https://www.optometry.org.au/institute-of-excellence/cpd-events/artificial-intelligence-scribes-in-practice-the-potential-for-ai-to-change-optometry).

Start by examining your current documentation process. Identify what you want help with, where the draft would enter your electronic health record, and who would review it.

Then evaluate a tool against those needs. The following approach gives owners a way to connect the technology to everyday practice operations.

## What an AI Scribe for Optometrists Does

### From spoken encounter to draft documentation

In an ambient workflow, the clinician activates a tool that captures the encounter conversation and uses it to prepare a note. Some products also offer dictation, allowing the clinician to supply a summary.

The American Psychological Association’s evaluation guide describes these approaches, along with built-in EHR tools, connected products, and standalone options.

Its guidance was developed for psychologists, so use these categories as a starting point for vendor questions. [APA evaluation guidance](https://www.apa.org/topics/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/evaluating-ai-scribes.html).

During a demonstration, follow the information through the entire process. Observe what starts capture, what ends it, where the draft appears, and how the clinician edits it.

Ask the vendor to distinguish demonstrated capabilities from features that would require additional setup.

For your own workflow, identify which information comes from conversation, which comes from testing, and which the clinician enters separately. Make that information map part of the evaluation.

### From draft documentation to a signed note

Build an explicit review step between note generation and finalization. The clinician should have a clear opportunity to inspect the draft, correct it, and confirm the assessment and plan before signing.

The American Medical Association’s decision framework addresses note accuracy, patient communication, privacy, consent, and clinician accountability.

These are useful categories for deciding how the scribe would operate inside your practice. [AMA decision framework](https://edhub.ama-assn.org/code-of-medical-ethics/module/2850594?bypassSolrId=J_2850594&resultClick=1).

Write down who owns each action. In a solo practice, the owner may select the product, establish the workflow, and review the notes. A group may distribute implementation responsibilities among clinical and operational leads.

In either setting, name the clinician responsible for reviewing each draft and define how staff can tell whether that review is complete.

## What the Evidence Can Tell a Practice Owner

### Eye-care research supports careful oversight

A study of AI scribing tools in simulated ophthalmic encounters reported: “All tools, however, exhibited errors.” The findings included omissions of critical details and support the need for clinician review.

The study concerns ophthalmology and simulated encounters, which limits what an optometry owner can conclude about everyday use in their own office. [Ophthalmology simulation study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41776297).

A separate commentary in *Eye* discusses potential documentation and patient-interaction benefits alongside accuracy, privacy, and oversight concerns.

It also identifies limited specialty-specific evidence. [Ophthalmology commentary](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41433-026-04346-y).

For an owner, this evidence supports a practical evaluation question: can your clinicians reliably review the output within the workflow you intend to use? Include that question in demonstrations and pilot discussions.

Assess the completed, reviewed record as well as the initial draft.

### Practice experience provides implementation context

In an *Optometry Times* discussion, an optometrist describes adopting an eye-care-specific EHR with an integrated AI scribe. The account offers a practice-level perspective on charting, staffing, and implementation challenges.

Use it to inform questions about adoption in your office while keeping its individual-practice context in view. [Optometry Times practice discussion](https://www.optometrytimes.com/view/the-independent-advantage-s02-e05-ai-emr-ehr-systems).

AOAExcel also describes an optometry EHR with an AI scribe for real-time charting. Its announcement discloses an investment in and endorsement of the company.

Read the product claims with that commercial relationship in mind. [AOAExcel announcement](https://www.aoa.org/practice/aoaexcel/aoaexcel-resource-updates/aoaexcel-news/aoaexcel-endorses-its-first-ever-ehr-barti-ehr).

Together, these sources offer different kinds of information: a clinician’s implementation experience and an association subsidiary’s product announcement.

Bring the resulting questions to your own evaluation, including training needs, chart structure, and the responsibilities assigned to staff.

## Evaluating How the Scribe Fits Your EHR

### Follow the complete documentation path

Ask the vendor to demonstrate the workflow using synthetic material that contains no real patient information. Begin with opening the encounter and continue through capture, draft generation, editing, transfer, and finalization.

Pay attention to the destination of each item. Where does the narrative appear? What happens to information intended for a structured field? Which steps require a person to move or re-enter content? Ask the demonstrator to show those actions on screen.

Include a correction in the demonstration. Have the clinician revise a draft before finalization and then inspect the resulting chart. Confirm where the revision appears and how a reviewer identifies the current version.

For a group, repeat the workflow against the configurations you actually plan to support. For a solo owner, concentrate on the templates and encounter types you use in your own office.

### Test the documentation tasks that matter to your practice

Prepare fictional encounter scripts around relevant documentation tasks, such as a routine examination discussion, contact lens follow-up, or explanation of a management plan. Keep the material entirely synthetic.

Decide in advance what you want to inspect. Your checklist might include whether the draft preserves laterality, separates historical information from current findings, and records the clinician’s stated plan.

Include a change of thought or clarification in the script so you can examine how the final meaning is represented.

Ask the optometrist who would use the tool to review the output. Give them room to explain what they would edit and why. Record the categories of editing required without including patient information.

The purpose of this exercise is to make the buying decision concrete: the owner should understand what the tool produces and what work remains for the practice.

## Making Clinical Review Part of the Workflow

### Review clinical meaning and completeness

Create a review checklist with your clinical lead. Organize it around the information the practice expects in a completed note.

Useful review prompts include:

- Does the history reflect what was discussed?
- Are examination findings represented in the intended context?
- Are right-eye and left-eye references correct?
- Does the assessment match the clinician’s judgment?
- Does the plan accurately describe the agreed next steps?
- Is any relevant information missing?

Treat these as prompts for clinician review, without assuming a particular product will make a particular error.

The published simulation study supports the broader concern about errors and omissions; your evaluation should establish how the selected tool behaves in your workflow.

Reserve time for this review during the pilot. Ask clinicians to distinguish substantive corrections from preferences about phrasing or formatting. That distinction gives the owner a clearer view of the work involved.

### Define what happens when the workflow breaks

Write a fallback procedure before routine use. Specify how the clinician completes documentation if capture fails, a draft is unavailable, or the output requires substantial correction.

Keep the procedure accessible to the people using the tool. Name the person who handles technical questions and the person who decides whether a clinical workflow should pause.

In a solo office, those responsibilities may sit with the owner and an office manager; a group can assign them across its support structure.

Also decide how to record recurring problems without circulating patient content. Use categories such as missing information, transfer problems, or template mismatch.

Discuss those categories during pilot reviews and route any necessary troubleshooting through the practice’s approved process.

## Addressing Privacy and Patient Communication

### Get written answers about data handling

The APA guide includes vendor reputation, functionality, privacy, security, and integration among its evaluation considerations.

Apply those topics to a written vendor questionnaire, with review by the people responsible for privacy and technology in your practice.

[APA vendor-evaluation guidance](https://www.apa.org/topics/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/evaluating-ai-scribes.html).

Ask what happens to audio, transcripts, and draft notes; where they are processed; who can access them; and how retention and deletion work.

Clarify whether other providers participate in processing and how information may be used beyond producing the note.

Have your designated advisers review the proposed arrangement and applicable requirements before introducing real patient information.

Keep the vendor’s answers with your evaluation materials, and identify which settings or contractual terms those answers depend on.

### Agree on how patients will hear about the tool

Prepare an explanation that matches the product and workflow you have reviewed. Describe what the tool does, how the clinician reviews the resulting note, and whom patients can ask for further information.

Have the practice’s designated advisers establish the appropriate disclosure and consent process. Assign responsibility for explaining the tool and documenting the relevant choice.

The AMA framework specifically includes patient communication, privacy, and consent as considerations for ambient AI use. [AMA ethics framework](https://edhub.ama-assn.org/code-of-medical-ethics/module/2850594?bypassSolrId=J_2850594&resultClick=1).

Rehearse the process with staff before launch. Include how the team responds when a patient has questions or prefers the established documentation workflow. Ensure staff know who can answer detailed questions about data handling.

## Running a Pilot That Informs the Decision

### Establish a baseline for the work you want to change

Before introducing a tool, describe the documentation problem in observable terms. Perhaps the owner wants to understand after-hours chart completion, repeated editing, or difficulty keeping notes organized.

Select the issue that matters in your practice and record how the current workflow operates.

A Stanford Health Care pilot evaluated ambient AI scribe use and documentation time in ambulatory care.

It provides a framework for measuring outcomes, although its findings are not specific to optometry. [Stanford documentation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11756633).

For your evaluation, include the full documentation process: preparing the draft, reviewing it, correcting it, and completing the chart. Record whether the tool was actually used and why clinicians chose the established workflow on other occasions.

Keep operational reporting free of patient information. Aggregate workflow observations and clinician feedback can structure the discussion without reproducing clinical notes.

### Define how you will decide to continue

Agree on the decision criteria before the pilot starts. Include clinical review, usability, integration, patient communication, training needs, and unresolved vendor questions.

Use a short review agenda:

- What did clinicians need to correct?
- Which steps required unexpected manual work?
- Could staff follow the agreed workflow?
- What questions remained unanswered?
- What would need to change before broader use?

Invite feedback from the people doing the work. A solo owner can hold this discussion with the staff involved in documentation and checkout. A group should hear from participating clinicians and local teams before extending the workflow.

Document whether the next step is continued evaluation, a workflow adjustment, broader adoption, or stopping use. Avoid expanding the pilot simply because access has already been purchased.

## Connecting the Scribe to Practice Operations

### Assign the handoffs around the clinical note

Map the operational actions that follow a reviewed plan, such as arranging follow-up, routing a referral task, or handling a patient question. Assign each action to a role and specify where that person receives the approved instruction.

Keep the status of draft and reviewed documentation clear in this handoff. Ask staff to demonstrate how they identify the instruction they should act on and where they send a question back to the clinician.

For group operators, define the shared handoff expectations while documenting any local differences. A solo owner can use the same approach in a simpler form: a written description of who receives the task, who completes it, and how completion is recorded.

This places the scribe within the wider work of [optometry practice operations](/industries/optometry/).

### Choose the next step around your actual bottleneck

Return to the problem that started the evaluation. If documentation is the concern, assess the reviewed note and the effort required to complete it.

If the concern is follow-up ownership, [phone coverage](/solutions/medical-answering-service/), or an unclear checkout process, make those needs explicit in the operating plan as well.

Before committing to broader use, review the proposed subscription, setup, training, support, and exit arrangements. Ask which responsibilities remain with your team and who will maintain the workflow when templates or product features change.

The decision should be understandable to the owner and the people expected to use the tool: what it will do, what they will still do, and what evidence will inform future changes.
