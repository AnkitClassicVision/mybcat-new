When you are acquiring your fifth or fifteenth practice, the capital structure you choose directly affects your EBITDA margins, PE attractiveness, and long-term valuation multiples. This guide breaks down the optometry practice financing loan options that actually work for groups managing 3 or more locations, with specific attention to SBA programs, conventional debt, and strategic financing structures that support scalable growth.

## Why Does Multi-Location Financing Require a Different Approach?

The average optometry practice generates approximately $947,000 in annual revenue with 8 employees. When you are managing 5 locations, you are not simply multiplying that profile by five. You are operating a platform with consolidated overhead, shared compliance costs, and centralized patient acquisition infrastructure that fundamentally changes your risk profile and capital needs.

Traditional lenders evaluate single-practice acquisitions using straightforward debt service coverage ratios. Multi-location groups present a more complex picture. Your consolidated financials show synergies that increase EBITDA margins, but they also reveal operational dependencies that create concentration risks. Lenders who specialize in [multi-location healthcare operations](/blog/optometry-network-operations-scale/) understand these dynamics and structure facilities accordingly.

The financing difference becomes stark when you compare acquisition approaches. A solo practitioner buying one practice needs a single loan with a fixed term and predictable payments. A growing optometry group needs a capital strategy that accommodates multiple acquisitions over 24 to 36 months, potentially including revolving credit facilities, delayed-draw term loans, and subordinated debt layers that preserve senior borrowing capacity.

Private equity interest in eye care has intensified this complexity. Over 40 transactions closed in 2024 and early 2025, with platforms like MyEyeDr. adding 30 practices to reach 869 total locations and AEG Vision targeting 80 acquisitions in 2025. These PE-backed competitors have access to institutional capital. Independent multi-location groups need sophisticated financing structures to compete.

For most multi-location groups, the optimal approach combines multiple financing types. A typical acquisition stack might include 70% SBA or conventional bank debt, 15% seller financing, and 15% buyer equity. This structure preserves cash for working capital while creating alignment between buyer and seller during the transition period.

Understanding practice valuation multiples is essential before selecting a financing structure. The debt terms you can secure depend heavily on how lenders assess the target practice's earnings quality and your group's ability to maintain or improve those earnings post-acquisition.

## How Do SBA 7(a) Loans Work for Multi-Location Optometry Groups?

The SBA 7(a) program remains the most common financing vehicle for healthcare practice acquisitions, including optometry. The program offers government-guaranteed loans up to $5 million with extended repayment terms that improve cash flow during the critical post-acquisition period.

Current interest rate structures for SBA 7(a) loans are tied to the prime rate. As of early 2026, with prime at 6.75%, maximum rates range from 9.75% for larger loans to 14.75% for loans under $25,000. Multi-location groups typically qualify for the most favorable rates due to their consolidated financial strength.

Multi-location groups can utilize multiple SBA loans simultaneously, though each loan is capped at $5 million and requires separate approval. Groups executing rapid acquisition strategies often establish relationships with multiple SBA preferred lenders to maintain deal velocity when multiple targets become available simultaneously.

The SBA guarantee (85% for loans under $150,000, 75% for larger loans) reduces lender risk and typically results in more favorable terms than purely conventional financing. However, SBA loans involve guarantee fees ranging from 2% to 3% of the guaranteed portion, which adds to the total cost of capital.

For groups executing their first few acquisitions, SBA financing typically offers the most favorable terms. The extended repayment periods (up to 10 years for acquisitions, 25 years if real estate is included) preserve cash flow during integration. This matters significantly when you are [optimizing EBITDA](/blog/multi-location-healthcare-ebitda/) across multiple locations simultaneously.

Groups with established banking relationships and strong consolidated financials often find conventional loans more efficient for subsequent acquisitions. When your lender understands your operations and has a track record of successful deals with your organization, the approval process accelerates. Some regional banks specializing in healthcare practice financing can close conventional loans in 10 to 14 business days.

The strategic consideration goes beyond individual deal economics. If your long-term plan includes PE partnership, the debt structure you build now affects your attractiveness to institutional capital. [PE-backed healthcare operations](/blog/pe-backed-healthcare-operations/) typically prefer clean capital structures with predictable terms that can be refinanced at the platform level.

## What Financing Terms Should Multi-Location Groups Expect?

Realistic expectations about financing terms help multi-location groups plan acquisitions without overcommitting to unsustainable debt service levels. Current market conditions for optometry group acquisitions reflect both the strength of the sector and the competitive lending environment.

Debt service coverage ratios (DSCR) remain the primary underwriting metric. Lenders typically require consolidated DSCR of 1.25x or higher, meaning your net operating income must exceed annual debt payments by 25%. For a $3 million acquisition with $300,000 in annual debt service, your post-acquisition EBITDA needs to exceed $375,000 to meet this threshold.

Multi-location groups with established operations often negotiate covenant packages that account for acquisition-related EBITDA fluctuations. A standard structure might include DSCR holiday periods of 6 to 12 months post-acquisition, allowing time for integration synergies to materialize before covenants take effect.

Loan-to-value requirements vary by lender and deal structure. SBA loans can provide up to 100% financing in certain circumstances, though most transactions involve 10% to 20% buyer equity. Conventional bank loans typically require 20% to 30% equity, though seller financing can satisfy a portion of this requirement in some structures.

The equipment component of practice acquisitions often receives separate financing treatment. Specialized optometry equipment (phoropters, OCT machines, autorefractors) can be financed through dedicated equipment loans that preserve your borrowing capacity for real estate and goodwill portions of acquisitions.

## How Does Seller Financing Fit Into Your Acquisition Strategy?

Seller financing creates alignment between buyer and seller that extends beyond the closing table. When the selling optometrist retains a financial interest in the practice's success, they have incentive to support patient retention, staff transitions, and referral network continuity during the critical post-acquisition period.

The typical seller financing structure involves 10% to 20% of the purchase price carried by the seller with a 3 to 7 year term and interest rates between 5% and 8%. These notes are typically subordinated to senior bank debt, meaning the bank gets paid first if the practice struggles.

For multi-location groups, seller financing serves multiple strategic purposes. First, it reduces the equity required from the buyer, preserving capital for subsequent acquisitions. Second, it creates a transition bridge that keeps the selling doctor engaged during the [90-day integration period](/blog/90-day-integration-playbook-healthcare-acquisitions/). Third, it signals to lenders that the seller has confidence in the practice's continued success.

Structuring seller notes requires attention to tax implications for both parties. Sellers often prefer installment sale treatment to spread capital gains recognition across multiple years. Buyers benefit from deductible interest payments. Working with counsel experienced in healthcare practice transactions ensures the note terms satisfy both parties' objectives while remaining enforceable.

The earnout variant of seller financing ties a portion of the purchase price to post-acquisition performance metrics. This structure reduces upfront risk for the buyer while potentially increasing total consideration for sellers who maintain strong patient retention and production during the earnout period. Groups acquiring practices where the selling doctor plans to continue practicing often find earnouts preferable to fixed seller notes.

## PE Alignment: Structuring Debt for Future Capital Partners

The private equity playbook in eye care has produced clear patterns that independent multi-location groups should understand when structuring their own capital. Over 313 PE deals worth $17 billion have closed in eye care globally since 2019. Understanding what makes platforms attractive to institutional capital helps you position your group accordingly.

PE investors evaluate acquisition financing structures during due diligence. Clean debt structures with straightforward terms, reasonable covenants, and manageable maturities receive favorable treatment. Complex debt stacks with multiple subordinated layers, cross-collateralization, and aggressive covenant packages create refinancing risk that reduces valuations.

The practical implication for multi-location groups: structure your acquisition debt with eventual refinancing in mind. Avoid prepayment penalties that extend beyond 3 to 5 years. Negotiate for portability provisions that allow debt assumption by a successor owner. Maintain detailed documentation of all loan terms, covenant calculations, and compliance history.

Understanding [due diligence requirements](/blog/dental-group-acquisition-due-diligence/) from the buyer's perspective helps you maintain the operational and financial records that make your group attractive to future capital partners. PE operating partners specifically evaluate debt covenant headroom, interest rate exposure, and refinancing risk when assessing platform opportunities.

The debt-to-EBITDA ratios that PE platforms accept have moderated from 2021 peaks but remain elevated compared to historical norms. Groups with 2.0x to 3.0x debt-to-EBITDA typically attract strong interest. Groups above 4.0x face refinancing questions that can complicate transactions.

## What Documentation Do Lenders Require for Group Acquisitions?

Multi-location optometry groups face more extensive documentation requirements than single-practice buyers. Lenders need to evaluate both the target practice and the acquiring platform, creating a two-sided underwriting process.

The target practice documentation package typically includes three years of tax returns and financial statements, accounts receivable aging reports, payer mix analysis, patient volume trends, equipment lists with depreciation schedules, lease agreements, and employment contracts. For practices transitioning from the selling doctor, production reports showing patient attribution become critical for assessing post-sale revenue risk.

The acquiring group's documentation requirements scale with complexity. Lenders require consolidated financial statements showing all existing locations, detailed cap tables identifying all owners and their guarantee obligations, organizational charts, existing debt schedules, and strategic plans outlining acquisition integration approaches.

Groups preparing for multiple acquisitions should establish a standardized due diligence protocol that captures all required documentation consistently across targets. This accelerates lender review and demonstrates operational sophistication. The [pre-sale operational cleanup](/blog/pre-sale-operational-cleanup-intake-valuation/) process matters on both sides of transactions.

Proactive groups maintain a standing data room with current financial statements, compliance documentation, and operational metrics that can be shared with lenders or potential capital partners on short notice. This readiness reduces deal timelines and demonstrates the professional management that lenders prefer.

## Operational Considerations That Affect Loan Approval

Lenders evaluate more than financial statements when underwriting multi-location optometry acquisitions. Operational factors significantly influence both approval decisions and loan terms. Understanding what lenders assess helps you position your group favorably.

Call answer rates and patient access metrics increasingly factor into lender evaluations. Groups that can demonstrate 95%+ answer rates across all locations signal operational discipline that protects revenue. Lenders recognize that missed calls translate directly to missed revenue. Implementing [centralized intake tracking](/blog/kpi-dashboard-multi-location-intake/) provides the documentation lenders want to see.

Staff retention metrics matter because optometry practices depend heavily on optician and technician continuity. Groups with turnover rates significantly above industry averages face questions about post-acquisition production sustainability. Document your retention programs and include turnover statistics in your loan package.

Insurance credentialing status affects revenue recognition timing. Groups that have streamlined credentialing processes for new acquisitions can demonstrate faster revenue ramp-up, supporting more aggressive production projections. Lenders familiar with healthcare operations understand credentialing delays and factor them into cash flow projections.

The integration track record of existing acquisitions provides the strongest evidence of operational capability. Groups that can show consistent EBITDA performance across previous acquisitions build lender confidence. Document your [integration playbook](/blog/healthcare-operations-ma-integration/) and share results from prior deals.

## Building a Multi-Year Acquisition Financing Strategy

Sustainable growth for multi-location optometry groups requires a financing strategy that extends beyond individual transactions. The capital structure decisions you make today constrain or enable your options for the next 3 to 5 years.

Start by establishing your target capital structure. Most successfully scaling optometry groups maintain debt-to-EBITDA ratios between 2.0x and 3.5x. Going higher accelerates growth but increases refinancing risk and may limit your options if market conditions shift. Going lower preserves flexibility but may result in slower growth than competitors.

Revolving credit facilities provide acquisition flexibility that term loans cannot match. A $2 million to $5 million revolver allows you to move quickly on opportunistic acquisitions without returning to lenders for each transaction. The unused commitment fees (typically 0.25% to 0.50% annually) represent reasonable insurance for deal readiness.

Consider your geographic concentration when planning acquisition financing. Lenders evaluate geographic diversification as a risk factor. Groups concentrated in a single metropolitan area face higher concentration risk assessments than groups spread across multiple markets. Your expansion geography affects available financing terms.

The relationship between operational improvement and financing capacity creates a virtuous cycle. Groups that demonstrate consistent [EBITDA improvement](/blog/multi-location-healthcare-ebitda/) across existing locations access better financing terms for new acquisitions. This makes operational excellence a financing strategy, not just an operational one.

Timing acquisition activity relative to existing debt maturities requires careful planning. Avoid situations where multiple loans mature within the same 12-month window. Staggered maturities provide refinancing flexibility and prevent forced refinancing during unfavorable market conditions.

For groups planning eventual PE partnership or sale, the financing structure you build should support that outcome. Work with advisors who understand [selling your optometry practice](/blog/guide-for-selling-your-optometry-practice/) from both the financing and transaction perspectives. The right capital structure today positions you optimally for tomorrow's options.

## Key Takeaways

Multi-location optometry group financing requires strategies that go far beyond what works for single-practice acquisitions. SBA 7(a) loans remain valuable tools for individual acquisitions, but growing groups need comprehensive capital strategies that combine multiple financing types, maintain PE-ready debt structures, and support multi-year acquisition plans. The groups that successfully scale their optometry platforms approach financing as a strategic function, not just a transactional necessity.

Current market conditions favor well-prepared acquirers. With over 40 eye care transactions closing in 2024 alone and major platforms continuing aggressive acquisition strategies, independent groups need access to competitive capital to remain viable. Building relationships with healthcare-focused lenders, maintaining pristine operational documentation, and structuring debt for eventual refinancing are the differentiators that separate successful scaling groups from those that stall.
