## What Are Optometry Practice Valuation Multiples in 2026?

Optometry practice valuation multiples in 2026 have stabilized after the volatility of 2022-2024, with EBITDA multiples ranging from 2.97x to 4.06x for typical transactions. High-performing practices with normalized EBITDA above $450,000 can achieve multiples between 4.66x and 5.14x according to recent transaction data. These figures represent a departure from the peak multiples seen during the 2021-2022 acquisition frenzy, reflecting a more disciplined buyer market focused on operational quality rather than growth at any cost.

For multi-location groups, the valuation equation becomes more complex. Platform-level organizations with $5M+ in aggregated EBITDA can command 10x to 12x multiples when structured as growth vehicles for PE sponsors, while add-on acquisitions typically trade in the 6x to 8x range. This tiered structure creates significant value arbitrage opportunities for groups that can position themselves as platforms rather than add-ons.

The SDE (Seller's Discretionary Earnings) multiple, commonly used for smaller practices, ranges from 1.96x to 2.67x in current market conditions. Revenue multiples have declined in relevance but still serve as sanity checks, typically falling between 0.43x and 0.75x of gross revenue. Some legacy valuation methods use 60-80% of gross revenue as a benchmark, though this approach fails to account for profitability variations between practices.

Understanding these baseline multiples is essential for [setting realistic expectations when selling your optometry practice](/blog/guide-for-selling-your-optometry-practice/). However, the raw multiple is just the starting point. What separates a 3x transaction from a 5x transaction comes down to operational execution, documentation quality, and strategic positioning.

## Why Do Multi-Location Groups Command Higher Multiples?

Multi-location optometry groups consistently command higher valuations than single practices because they reduce buyer risk while offering growth infrastructure that PE sponsors actively seek. A group with standardized operations across five locations presents a fundamentally different investment thesis than five independent practices with the same aggregate revenue.

The premium stems from several interconnected factors. Geographic diversification reduces reliance on any single market, insulating the business from local economic downturns or competitive pressures. Centralized operations create margin expansion opportunities through shared services, volume purchasing, and standardized protocols. Existing management infrastructure reduces post-acquisition integration costs, which can consume 15-25% of transaction value in add-on scenarios.

PE sponsors specifically target multi-location groups because they serve as platforms for future roll-ups. A 5-location optometry group with proven integration capabilities becomes the foundation for acquiring 15 additional practices over a 3-5 year hold period. This platform premium can add 2x to 4x to baseline multiples when the right operational elements are in place.

The data supports this premium structure. According to Physician Growth Partners' Q1 2025 analysis, ophthalmology practices (which share many operational characteristics with optometry) command 7x to 10x EBITDA multiples due to higher procedure volumes and integrated service models. Optometry groups approaching similar operational maturity can capture portions of this premium through strategic positioning.

For operations leaders managing multi-location groups, understanding the [relationship between EBITDA performance and healthcare group valuations](/blog/multi-location-healthcare-ebitda/) becomes critical when preparing for strategic discussions.

## How Do Operational Metrics Affect Your Multiple?

Operational metrics separate commodity practices from premium assets in optometry group valuations. While financial performance sets the baseline, buyers apply significant multiple adjustments based on operational quality indicators that predict post-acquisition performance and integration risk.

Patient retention rates serve as the primary operational indicator. Groups maintaining 80%+ annual patient retention demonstrate the recurring revenue characteristics that justify premium valuations. Declining patient counts create discounts of 0.5x to 1.0x on baseline multiples, as buyers model the cost of rebuilding the patient base post-acquisition.

Capture rates for eyewear and contact lenses directly impact revenue per patient, a metric that correlates strongly with profitability. Groups achieving 55%+ capture rates on optical products command higher multiples because they demonstrate effective revenue optimization without relying on exam volume growth alone. Low capture rates signal operational inefficiencies that require post-acquisition investment to correct.

Recall program effectiveness has emerged as a critical valuation factor for multi-location groups. Practices with [centralized patient recall systems](/blog/centralized-patient-recall-multi-location/) demonstrate the operational infrastructure buyers seek. A recall rate above 75% indicates strong patient engagement systems, while rates below 50% suggest significant revenue leakage that depresses valuations.

Staff productivity metrics, measured as revenue per FTE or patients per OD, reveal operational efficiency that translates directly to margin potential. Groups with high staff productivity can scale without proportional headcount increases, creating the margin expansion PE sponsors target.

## Which Due Diligence Factors Drive Premium Valuations?

Due diligence in multi-location optometry transactions goes far beyond financial audits. Buyers evaluate transferability, scalability, and integration risk through a comprehensive review that touches every operational function. Understanding what buyers examine helps groups prepare documentation and address weaknesses before entering the market.

Financial cleanliness ranks as the primary due diligence concern. Clean financials mean accurate reporting, consistent accounting practices across locations, and minimal adjustments needed to arrive at normalized EBITDA. Groups requiring extensive restatements or showing inconsistent practices between locations face valuation discounts and extended due diligence timelines.

Lease term analysis affects both valuation and deal structure. Favorable leases with 5+ years remaining and reasonable renewal terms support premium valuations. Short-term leases or above-market rental rates create contingent liabilities that buyers either discount from purchase price or require sellers to remediate pre-close.

OD dependency presents a significant risk factor for multi-location groups. Practices where specific doctors generate disproportionate revenue face transferability concerns. Buyers discount heavily for key-person risk, often requiring employment agreements or earnout structures that shift risk back to sellers. Groups with balanced revenue distribution across ODs and locations command premiums.

Technology infrastructure evaluation has intensified as buyers prioritize integration speed. Groups operating unified PM/EHR systems across locations present lower integration risk than those running multiple platforms. According to Sofer Advisors' 2025-2026 medical practice valuation guide, technology standardization can add 0.25x to 0.5x to baseline multiples by reducing post-acquisition integration costs.

The [pre-sale operational cleanup process](/blog/pre-sale-operational-cleanup-intake-valuation/) should begin 12-24 months before marketing a transaction, allowing time to address documentation gaps, standardize systems, and build the operational track record buyers expect.

## How Do You Calculate Portfolio Value Across Locations?

Calculating portfolio value for multi-location optometry groups requires location-level analysis aggregated into a unified valuation framework. Simple multiplication of per-practice multiples underestimates portfolio value for operationally integrated groups while overestimating value for loosely affiliated practices.

The standard approach begins with normalized EBITDA calculation for each location, applying consistent add-back methodologies across the portfolio. Add-backs typically include owner/OD compensation above market rates, one-time expenses, personal expenses flowing through the business, and related-party transactions. Consistency in these adjustments prevents due diligence disputes.

Portfolio-level adjustments then apply to the aggregated EBITDA. Positive adjustments include shared services savings already realized, demonstrated integration capabilities, management depth, and geographic positioning. Negative adjustments account for technology fragmentation, key-person concentration, and operational inconsistencies between locations.

## The 12-24 Month Value Optimization Roadmap

Maximizing optometry group valuations requires systematic preparation beginning 12-24 months before marketing the transaction. This timeline allows sufficient runway to address operational gaps, build documentation, and demonstrate the performance consistency buyers require for premium multiples.

**Months 1-6: Foundation and Audit Phase**

Begin with a comprehensive operational audit across all locations. Document current state metrics for patient retention, capture rates, recall compliance, and staff productivity. Identify variance between locations and prioritize standardization efforts. Engage accounting resources to clean financial records and establish consistent reporting across the portfolio.

Review lease terms across all locations and initiate renegotiation or extension discussions where needed. Address any deferred maintenance or equipment needs that could create contingent liability concerns during due diligence.

**Months 7-12: Standardization Phase**

Implement standardized operating procedures across all locations, focusing on patient intake, recall programs, and revenue cycle management. Groups that have [scaled their optometry network operations](/blog/optometry-network-operations-scale/) demonstrate the operational maturity that commands premium valuations.

Centralize functions where feasible, including patient recall, insurance verification, and appointment scheduling. These shared services create margin improvement that directly impacts EBITDA while demonstrating integration capabilities buyers value.

Address key-person dependencies by cross-training staff and documenting institutional knowledge. Reduce OD concentration by balancing patient loads across providers.

**Months 13-24: Performance and Documentation Phase**

Focus on demonstrating consistent performance across the standardized operational model. Build at least 12 months of clean financial records under the new structure. Document all processes, creating the operations manual that facilitates post-acquisition integration.

Engage transaction advisors and begin preliminary buyer outreach. The final six months should focus on marketing preparation, management presentations, and buyer qualification. Groups entering the market with strong documentation and demonstrated performance consistency compress deal timelines and achieve better outcomes.

## Common Valuation Mistakes Multi-Location Groups Make

Multi-location optometry groups frequently leave value on the table through avoidable mistakes in preparation and positioning. Understanding these pitfalls helps operations leaders maximize transaction outcomes.

**Inconsistent financial reporting across locations** creates immediate credibility concerns for buyers. When Location A uses one chart of accounts while Location B uses another, due diligence becomes contentious and time-consuming. Buyers either discount for the additional work or walk away entirely. Standardizing financial reporting 18+ months pre-transaction eliminates this issue.

**Underestimating integration costs of technology fragmentation** leads to valuation adjustments sellers find surprising. Running three different PM systems across five locations might feel manageable operationally, but buyers see 6-12 months of integration work and significant migration risk. The discount for technology fragmentation often exceeds the cost of proactive consolidation.

**Overreliance on owner-OD production** triggers key-person concerns that depress multiples. When 40%+ of group revenue depends on selling owners, buyers model significant revenue risk post-close. Building OD depth and distributing patient relationships across multiple providers addresses this concern.

**Neglecting patient retention infrastructure** signals operational weakness that sophisticated buyers recognize. Groups without systematic recall programs, reactivation campaigns, or retention tracking leave obvious value on the table. The [ROI of patient reactivation](/blog/patient-reactivation-roi/) demonstrates how these programs impact both current operations and transaction valuations.

**Entering the market without competitive tension** surrenders negotiating position. Groups that approach a single buyer or platform without running a competitive process consistently achieve lower multiples than those with multiple qualified bidders. Transaction advisors provide access to buyer networks and create the competitive dynamics that drive premium outcomes.

**Poor documentation of operational processes** increases perceived integration risk. Buyers pay premiums for groups that can hand over operations manuals, training materials, and documented procedures. Groups relying on tribal knowledge and undocumented practices face discounts and extended transition periods.
