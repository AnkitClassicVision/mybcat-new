An eye care chair time playbook should explain how appointment demand, clinical resources, administrative preparation, and visit flow fit together.

For a COO or VP of Operations overseeing 3+ locations, that means defining what schedulers may book, what each location can support, who handles exceptions, and how leaders evaluate changes.

Start with a practical operating question: **What must be ready for the next appropriate patient visit to proceed as planned?** Answer it across the entire journey, from the appointment request through the clinical visit and checkout.

This playbook provides a framework for making those decisions. Use it to document shared standards, preserve necessary local differences, and test scheduling changes against observed workflows.

## Define Chair Time Across the Group

### Establish a shared operating vocabulary

For this playbook, define chair time as the clinical appointment capacity your group plans, prepares, and uses. Document the distinction between scheduled appointment duration, clinician contact time, room occupancy, and total time in the practice. Give each measure a clear start and end event.

Also distinguish between capacity that is published for booking, capacity deliberately reserved for approved purposes, and time unavailable because a required resource is absent. Ask location managers to use those definitions when explaining their schedules.

The American Optometric Association discusses chair-cost calculations, scheduling methods, and planning for no-shows to avoid wasting “valuable chair-time.” Its guidance provides a foundation for connecting appointment management with practice economics.

[Source: AOA day-to-day operations](https://www.aoa.org/practice/new-doctors-of-optometry/day-to-day-operations).

### Document local constraints within a common template

Create a capacity profile for each location. Include available appointment families, clinician coverage, technician responsibilities, diagnostic equipment, room availability, and any approved restrictions on booking.

Record the reason for each local difference. A location may need a distinct template because its available services or shared resources differ. Require an owner and review date for those exceptions.

Use this profile during scheduling reviews and acquisition integration. Before adopting another location’s appointment template, have the local clinical and operations leads confirm that the same assumptions apply. The approval should identify which rules can transfer and which require local testing.

## Map the Visit Before Changing the Schedule

### Observe clinical and administrative handoffs

AHRQ’s workflow toolkit explicitly addresses “clinical and administrative workflow” in ambulatory care. It offers a framework for examining how technology and workflow changes affect the way work moves through a practice.

[Source: AHRQ workflow assessment toolkit](https://digital.ahrq.gov/health-it-tools-and-resources/evaluation-resources/workflow-assessment-health-it-toolkit).

Apply that approach by mapping an appointment from initial request through departure. At each transition, document the sending role, receiving role, required information, and signal that the next task can begin.

Include administrative preparation, check-in, technician work, diagnostic testing when ordered, clinician assessment, and checkout or optical handoff when applicable. Have staff validate the map against actual work. Record where the written procedure and observed sequence differ.

### Investigate where the queue forms

A peer-reviewed ophthalmology quality-improvement study used a “time-in-motion study,” process mapping, and root-cause analysis to investigate clinic bottlenecks.

It evaluated scheduling amendments, dedicated postoperative clinics, and documentation templates against visit duration and service capacity. [Source: ophthalmology workflow study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7792026).

For your own review, distinguish time spent performing a task from time spent waiting for the next task. Ask whether a delay begins at arrival, during preparation, before testing, before clinician assessment, or during departure.

Write a specific problem statement before selecting a change. For example, investigate whether appointment arrivals coincide with demand for the same testing resource. Assign an observation owner and define what evidence would support or challenge that explanation.

## Build Appointment Templates Around Required Resources

### Define clinically approved appointment families

Ask clinical leadership to approve the appointment families schedulers may use. For each family, document the booking criteria, expected resource requirements, preparation instructions, and escalation route when the request does not fit.

Research on ophthalmology scheduling developed templates using “simulation models” and electronic health record data.

It supports evaluating appointment design against workflow and visit characteristics. [Source: data-driven ophthalmology scheduling research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6391189).

Use that evidence as a reason to examine your own visit patterns. Set appointment durations through local clinical and operational review. Document which requests require clinical clarification before an appointment type can be selected, and give schedulers a defined route for obtaining that clarification.

### Review the sequence of shared-resource demand

Optometric Management describes a “level-loaded” scheduling approach that considers appointment mix, staggered doctor starts, technician workloads, diagnostic equipment, and optical handoffs.

Its guidance addresses how the schedule distributes work across shared resources. [Source: patient-flow guidance](https://optometricmanagement.com/issues/2014/june/business-efficiency).

Review a proposed template as a sequence of resource demands. Ask when patients are expected to need technician support, testing equipment, clinician attention, and optical assistance.

Before publication, walk through the template with representatives from each affected role. Record unresolved dependencies and assign responsibility for resolving them.

If the proposed sequence requires staffing or equipment that is unavailable, revise the proposal before opening those appointments for booking.

## Make Patient Access Responsible for Booking Readiness

### Write a booking-ready standard

Give centralized and local scheduling teams a shared definition of a booking-ready appointment. Specify the administrative information, approved visit category, location selection, preparation communication, and unresolved-task ownership expected at booking.

Define how staff should handle uncertainty. A question about an appointment category should have a named escalation route. A request that requires clinical judgment should follow the group’s clinician-approved process.

Document the handoff for any work that remains open after booking. The receiving team should be able to see the task, its owner, and its current status within the group’s approved systems.

For the broader operating model, connect this standard with your [centralized optometry scheduling standards](/blog/centralized-optometry-scheduling-standards/).

### Govern cancellations and replacement appointments

The AOA includes no-show planning within appointment management. Use that guidance to establish a documented process for reviewing newly available appointments and deciding whether they are appropriate for replacement booking.

[Source: AOA appointment-management guidance](https://www.aoa.org/practice/new-doctors-of-optometry/day-to-day-operations).

Specify who releases a canceled appointment, which appointment families may use it, and when location approval is required. Include preparation requirements and resource availability in that decision.

Track each operational event distinctly: an opening became available, outreach occurred, an appointment was booked, and a visit was completed. Describe each event accurately in management reporting. Give unsuccessful outreach and unresolved booking questions an explicit next action.

## Assign Decision Rights and Exception Ownership

### Name the accountable roles

Have the buying committee approve ownership before introducing a new scheduling tool or external support team. Document who controls appointment templates, who maintains booking instructions, and who authorizes departures from the standard.

A practical allocation is to assign clinical leadership responsibility for visit requirements and clinical escalation rules; operations leadership responsibility for workflow standards and rollout decisions; location managers responsibility for local readiness; and patient-access leadership responsibility for booking execution.

Finance and analytics should own the definitions used in financial and operational reporting. Name the person responsible for reconciling disagreements between local reports and the group dashboard.

Publish these responsibilities alongside the playbook so staff can identify the decision owner during daily work.

### Make exceptions reviewable

Create an exception register for deviations that require review. Use operational categories such as appointment-type uncertainty, unavailable equipment, staffing changes, booking overrides, preparation gaps, or incomplete handoffs.

Each entry should identify the rule involved, the responsible role, the action taken, and whether the issue remains open. Keep leadership reporting aggregated and exclude patient-identifying details.

During review, ask whether the event reflects an unclear rule, an execution problem, or a local constraint. Assign the corresponding action: clarify the standard, address the execution issue, or approve a documented exception. Close the entry only after the owner has recorded the resolution.

## Build a Chair-Time Scorecard With Clear Definitions

### Pair appointment activity with visit-flow measures

Choose measures that answer specific operating questions. Before publishing a dashboard, document each measure’s source, start and end events, exclusions, reporting owner, and review purpose.

| Measure | Question for the operating review | Definition to establish |
|---|---|---|
| Published appointment capacity | What was available for booking? | Included sessions and unavailable-time rules |
| Unused appointment time | Which openings remained unused? | Treatment of cancellations, reserved time, and closures |
| Booking exceptions | Where did staff depart from approved rules? | Categories, approval requirements, and resolution status |
| Preparation status | What administrative work remained at arrival? | Required tasks and completion criteria |
| Visit-stage timing | Where did waiting or task time occur? | Consistent start and end events |
| Session completion | When did planned clinical work finish? | Session boundaries and treatment of unfinished work |

Review comparable appointment families and operating conditions together. Annotate staffing changes, service changes, and template revisions so readers can understand the context of each reporting period.

### Connect operational findings with financial context

Optometric Management discusses efficiency in a “large-scale multi-location practice” through revenue per patient and revenue per working hour.

It connects those measures with scheduling, delegation, and patient waiting time. [Source: practice-metrics guidance](https://optometricmanagement.com/issues/2020/september/build-out-your-metrics).

Ask finance to define the accounting basis, reporting period, included revenue, and working-hour denominator before comparing locations. Label those definitions clearly.

Use financial measures alongside the workflow review. When a measure changes, examine appointment mix, staffing, available sessions, and operational changes before drawing a conclusion. Give the committee an explanation of the observed conditions and the questions still requiring investigation.

## Pilot Changes With an Explicit Decision Rule

### Test a defined operational change

EyeNet describes ophthalmic practices using revised scheduling, remote scribing, and electronic patient tracking as part of efficiency work. It emphasizes empowering staff to “gather metrics” when evaluating revised procedures.

[Source: EyeNet lean-practice article](https://www.ophth.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Lean-Practice-Management-_-EyeNet-Magazine-July-2021.pdf).

Translate that approach into a bounded pilot. Select a location and appointment family with a documented problem. Describe the proposed change, the operational question it addresses, and the observations needed to evaluate it.

For example, a pilot could examine a revised appointment sequence or a clearer booking-to-location handoff. Have affected staff confirm the instructions before launch. Identify who can pause the pilot and how the prior approved workflow would be restored.

### Decide what qualifies for expansion

Set the review criteria before the pilot begins. Include workflow observations, staff feedback, booking exceptions, preparation status, and the relevant scorecard measures.

Require a written decision to retain, revise, or stop the change. Explain the decision using the agreed criteria, and record any unresolved questions. If staffing or service availability changed during the pilot, include that context in the review.

Before expansion, compare the receiving location’s capacity profile with the pilot’s assumptions. Confirm clinical approval, staff training, system configuration, and exception ownership.

Use the [centralized scheduling rollout guide](/blog/centralized-scheduling-rollout-multi-location-practices/) to coordinate the wider implementation.

## Put the Playbook Into Daily Operations

### Give each review a concrete purpose

Use the daily location huddle to review staffing, equipment availability, preparation gaps, and appointments requiring clarification. Assign an owner to each open issue.

Use the recurring operations review to examine patterns: repeated overrides, unresolved handoffs, appointment categories requiring clarification, and differences between planned and observed workflows. Decide which issues need a procedure change and which need local follow-up.

Keep the published playbook current. Each revision should show its owner, approval date, affected locations, and training requirements. Ask managers to verify that scheduling instructions and system settings reflect the approved version.

### Prepare an enterprise discussion around actual decisions

For leaders overseeing **3+ locations**, bring the current appointment rules, location capacity profiles, ownership map, and an aggregated view of unresolved workflow issues to the next planning discussion.

Use those materials to identify the administrative work under consideration, the decisions retained by clinical leadership, and the evidence needed to assess a proposed operating model.
