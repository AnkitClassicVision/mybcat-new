Published optometry practice valuation benchmarks provide reference points for acquisition discussions. Review of Optometric Business articles published in 2025 describe ranges of “4–6x EBITDA” and “3-6x EBITDA” in their respective valuation and private equity discussions. Those are attributed observations from dated publications, rather than a current appraisal of a particular practice group. [Financial-records valuation discussion](https://reviewob.com/how-incomplete-practice-financials-overvalued-a-practice-by-300k-and-almost-blew-up-a-transition), [private equity valuation discussion](https://reviewob.com/private-equity).

For an owner, COO, VP of Operations, or buying committee evaluating a practice or group, the practical question is how to connect a published benchmark to the business being acquired. Which earnings are supported? Which expenses continue after the seller leaves? What equipment, lease, and operating commitments need review?

Use the benchmarks to organize those questions. Then build a practice-specific assessment around financial records and operating evidence.

## What the published valuation benchmarks show

### EBITDA ranges reported in industry publications

The sources below offer different perspectives on valuation. Keep the publication date and transaction context attached to each observation.

| Publication | Published observation | Application to group diligence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Review of Optometric Business, 2025 financial-records article | Cites a typical range of “4–6x EBITDA” and discusses earnings-based, market-based, and asset-based approaches. | Examine the documentation supporting the earnings used in a valuation. |
| Review of Optometric Business, 2025 private equity article | Reports multiples around “3-6x EBITDA” in its discussion of private equity buyers. | Identify the buyer context behind a proposed comparison. |
| Review of Optometric Business, 2018 industry report | Compares traditional practice transactions with consolidator approaches and discusses platform and tuck-in acquisitions. | Use its strategic distinctions as historical context. |

The observations come from the [financial-records article](https://reviewob.com/how-incomplete-practice-financials-overvalued-a-practice-by-300k-and-almost-blew-up-a-transition), [private equity article](https://reviewob.com/private-equity), and [historical consolidation report](https://www.reviewob.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/9-27-18pe.pdf).

### Match each benchmark to its publication and buyer context

Do not combine the published ranges into a new average or describe them as verified current transaction data. These sources do not establish a current market clearing price for your group.

For internal comparison, record the source date, buyer type, valuation approach, and earnings definition beside every benchmark. Where a publication leaves a detail unspecified, mark it as unknown. Ask your valuation adviser to assess whether the comparison fits the transaction under consideration.

A committee should be able to distinguish an industry article’s observation from the evidence supporting its own offer. That distinction is especially useful when a presentation includes an attractive multiple without explaining the underlying practice or earnings adjustments.

## Build a defensible earnings baseline

### Document the path from reported earnings to adjustments

The 2025 financial-records article describes a $300,000 valuation disagreement involving personal and discretionary expense add-backs that the seller could not substantiate. Its lesson for diligence is specific: claimed adjustments need supporting records. [Review of Optometric Business](https://reviewob.com/how-incomplete-practice-financials-overvalued-a-practice-by-300k-and-almost-blew-up-a-transition).

Create a reconciliation that starts with reported results and lists each proposed adjustment separately. For every entry, identify the accounting record, the business explanation, the supporting document, and the reviewer’s disposition. Keep disputed items visible.

Use separate columns for seller-proposed adjustments and buyer-accepted adjustments. Ask finance to explain any difference before the committee discusses the multiple. This makes the negotiation’s assumptions reviewable without presenting an unresolved earnings figure as agreed.

For a proposed discretionary-expense adjustment, request both the original expense record and an explanation of why the expense would cease. For a proposed shared-service change, request the implementation assumptions and the budget owner’s review. Keep those supporting explanations attached to the relevant line item so reviewers can challenge the reasoning.

For groups with shared services, include the treatment of central expenses in that reconciliation. The committee should be able to follow the same expense from the underlying records into the consolidated presentation.

### Account for the work that continues after the sale

The American Optometric Association identifies overhead, financial records, equipment, leases, growth, and goodwill among the considerations in evaluating an acquisition. Its guidance also calls for an accountant to review the records. [AOA buying guidance](https://www.aoa.org/practice/owning-a-practice/buying-a-practice).

Turn those categories into an operating review. Ask who performs the seller’s clinical, supervisory, administrative, and relationship-management work. Identify the responsibilities that remain necessary after the transition and the staffing assumptions used in the financial model.

If the model assumes that group personnel will absorb responsibilities, ask the relevant operating leader to review that assumption. Document the work, proposed owner, available capacity, and unresolved questions. Require supporting estimates for proposed staffing changes.

Keep this discussion concrete. A description such as “the owner handles exceptions” needs a workflow explanation before the committee can evaluate the associated operating plan.

## Evaluate the group location by location

### Reconcile site results with group financials

For multi-location diligence, request a consistent financial view for each site alongside consolidated results. Use matching reporting periods and explain differences in account classifications, shared-service allocations, and expense treatment.

Ask finance and operations to review the presentation together. Finance can trace the records; operating leaders can explain how the staffing and workflow assumptions relate to each location. Where a site depends on another location’s personnel or central support, describe that dependency explicitly.

A useful comparison worksheet includes the reporting period, responsible manager, allocation method, unresolved accounting questions, and operating assumptions requiring review. Avoid filling gaps with a group-wide assumption simply because the information is difficult to collect.

Pay particular attention to the boundary between location expenses and central support. Ask whether each site presentation includes the same categories of management, scheduling, and administrative work. Where allocations differ, explain the method and have finance reconcile the location schedules to the consolidated accounts. Retain that explanation in the diligence file.

For a deeper discussion of reporting structure, see [accounting for multi-location optometry groups](/blog/accounting-for-optometry-groups-multi-location/).

### Define the group’s role in an acquisition strategy

The 2018 consolidation report discusses platform and tuck-in acquisitions, geographic proximity, aggregation, and the relationship between scale and earnings multiples. Its numerical benchmarks are historical. [Private Equity Takes Root](https://www.reviewob.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/9-27-18pe.pdf).

Use those distinctions to ask what the buyer intends to acquire. Is the group expected to provide management capabilities for further acquisitions? Or will its locations join an existing operating organization? State the intended role before selecting a comparison.

If the investment thesis depends on an established management structure, request evidence of that structure: responsibility assignments, reporting routines, escalation paths, and documented decisions. If the thesis depends on integration into the buyer’s organization, describe the work and resources the buyer expects to contribute.

Treat “platform” as a proposition to examine. Ask the deal team to explain the capabilities behind the label and how those capabilities appear in its valuation reasoning.

## Review cash needs alongside the multiple

### Examine working capital and equipment requirements

Optometric Management’s 2024 transaction article identifies working capital, capital expenditures, and expense add-backs as valuation considerations. It asks, regarding capital expenses, “What is required to bring the practice up to standards?” [Transaction pitfalls](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2024/novemberdecember/preventing-practice-sales-transaction-pitfalls).

For the acquisition under review, request an equipment inventory, available maintenance information, lease commitments, and the proposed operating cash assumptions. Have the relevant reviewers identify the items that need further investigation.

Keep equipment and facilities questions attached to the affected location. Ask whether the financial model includes the operating plan’s proposed replacements or changes, and document the answer.

Where an estimate remains unresolved, describe the uncertainty in the committee materials. Assign someone to obtain evidence rather than inserting an unsupported allowance or assuming that the issue is immaterial.

### Keep financing and market context visible

The same article discusses interest rates and buyer competition as factors affecting practice valuation. It also explains why larger buyers emphasize EBITDA and cash flow. [Optometric Management](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2024/novemberdecember/preventing-practice-sales-transaction-pitfalls).

In your decision materials, identify the financing assumptions and market comparisons supplied by the transaction team. Ask when each was prepared and whether the adviser considers it applicable to the proposed acquisition.

Use a consistent set of assumptions when comparing alternatives. If competing analyses use different earnings adjustments, equipment plans, or operating cash requirements, reconcile those differences before presenting their conclusions side by side.

Avoid treating a published multiple as an answer to those transaction-specific questions. Ask the committee to review the benchmark, the earnings support, and the operating requirements together.

## Make operational diligence part of valuation preparation

### Document front-office ownership and dependencies

Build an operating map for scheduling, call handling, recall, insurance-verification handoffs, and unresolved service requests. For each workflow, identify the responsible team, escalation owner, location-specific rules, and systems involved.

Use workflow descriptions and aggregate reporting in the committee presentation. Keep patient-identifying information out of valuation slides and operating summaries.

The purpose of this exercise is to make the operating assumptions explicit. If a model assumes centralized scheduling, show which locations use that workflow today and which would require a change. If a manager handles exceptions informally, document the decision rules the acquisition team needs to understand.

Ask location leaders to review the map. Record disagreements and open questions so that a polished group-level presentation does not conceal an unresolved local dependency.

The [front-office standardization guide for optometry groups](/blog/optometry-front-office-standardization-group-practices/) offers related operating considerations.

### Separate current operations from proposed integration changes

Label each initiative according to its status: currently operating, approved for implementation, or proposed for evaluation. Include the supporting records, accountable owner, and implementation dependencies.

For valuation discussions, keep existing financial results and proposed operating changes in separately identified parts of the model. Ask the transaction advisers to explain how they have treated each assumption.

For example, a proposed centralized call-handling model needs a description of staffing, routing, escalation, training, and reporting responsibilities. If those decisions remain open, record them as open. Do not attach an invented valuation premium or financial improvement to the proposal.

Apply the same discipline to systems changes, shared services, and management restructuring. A committee should be able to tell what it is acquiring today and what its own team is proposing to implement after closing.

## Prepare the buying committee for a decision

### Write a memo that makes the assumptions reviewable

Organize the valuation memo around the evidence the committee needs to evaluate. Include the dated benchmark references, the reported financial baseline, proposed adjustments, site-level reconciliation, and outstanding operating questions.

Attach the source or responsible reviewer to each material assumption. Where the seller and buyer disagree, state both positions and identify the evidence needed to resolve them.

Ask committee members to review the earnings reconciliation before debating where a proposed transaction belongs relative to a published range. Then review the operating plan and cash requirements supporting the proposal.

The AOA describes goodwill as “potential income that buyers can reasonably expect after a practice changes hand.” Use that definition to frame questions about continuity, responsibilities, and the assumptions behind the acquisition case. [AOA buying guidance](https://www.aoa.org/practice/owning-a-practice/buying-a-practice).

### Turn open operating questions into an action plan

Before the decision meeting ends, assign an owner and next action to every unresolved operating assumption. Specify the evidence required and the person responsible for reviewing it. Bring unresolved valuation questions back to the transaction advisers with that supporting context.

For a practice of any size, a useful next step is an operating-readiness discussion focused on front-office responsibilities, central support, and location-specific handoffs. Bring the workflow map, proposed changes, and questions requiring management decisions.
