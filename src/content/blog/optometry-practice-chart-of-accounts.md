An optometry practice chart of accounts is the organized list of categories used to record the practice’s financial activity. It gives each transaction a defined place in your books, from professional services and optical sales to payroll, rent, equipment, and owner equity.

For an owner doing the books, the practical challenge is deciding how much detail to keep. A broad “supplies” account leaves important questions unanswered. A separate account for every vendor creates a long report without necessarily making it more useful.

Start with categories that reflect how you operate the practice, then write clear rules for using them. Whether you manage a solo office or a group, the aim is a report you can read, explain, and compare over time.

The structure below is a suggested starting point for discussion with your accountant. Adapt it to your services, accounting method, and ownership structure before changing your books.

## Build the Chart Around the Questions You Need to Answer

### Make clinical, optical, and operating activity recognizable

Start by listing the questions you bring to a financial review. You may want to understand how clinical revenue compares with product sales, which expenses belong to the optical department, or what you spend on staffing and facilities.

Those questions should guide your account structure. In “Use Metrics in Your Practice,” Optometric Management recommends organizing the chart around major expense areas and comparing the practice’s results over time. Its reminder that “Each practice is different” is useful when deciding which categories deserve additional detail. [Source: Optometric Management](https://optometricmanagement.com/issues/2010/november/use-metrics-in-your-practice)

For your own chart, give an account a clear purpose before adding it. “Contact lens product sales” answers a recognizable reporting question. A vaguely named “Other sales” account needs an explanation of what belongs there.

Keep the same discipline for expenses. If you want to review software separately from outside administrative services, establish those categories before transactions accumulate.

### Include the balance sheet in your design

A chart of accounts also needs categories for assets, liabilities, and equity. Reviewing only revenue and expenses leaves that part of the structure unfinished.

Vision One Credit Union describes both optometry-specific income and expense categories and balance-sheet items such as cash, inventory, equipment, accounts payable, debt, and equity. Its guidance emphasizes that “The goal is clarity.” [Source: Vision One Credit Union](https://www.visionone.org/education/financial-statements)

Ask your accountant to define the balance-sheet accounts your practice needs, including any related accounts for depreciation, payroll obligations, or owner transactions. Give these accounts descriptions just as you would for revenue and expenses.

For everyday bookkeeping, the useful question is simple: does this transaction belong in an operating category, or does it need a balance-sheet treatment? Put uncertain items on a review list instead of choosing an expense account merely because cash left the bank.

## A Sample Optometry Practice Chart of Accounts

### Balance-sheet account groups

Use the following table as a planning worksheet. Each suggested account should exist only where it fits your practice’s accounting setup. The review column identifies decisions to settle with your accountant before implementation.

| Account group | Suggested accounts | Definition to establish |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | Operating checking; savings; other business bank accounts | Which actual account each ledger account represents |
| Receivables | Accounts receivable, where applicable | How balances connect to the practice’s reporting system |
| Inventory | Frames; contact lenses; other products held for sale | How inventory records and accounting entries connect |
| Prepaid expenses and deposits | Prepaid expenses; refundable deposits | Which items belong here and how they are reviewed |
| Fixed assets | Clinical equipment; office equipment; furniture; improvements | How purchases and related accounting adjustments are recorded |
| Payables and other obligations | Accounts payable; credit cards; payroll liabilities | Which statement or supporting record substantiates each balance |
| Borrowings | Separate accounts for individual loans | How payments and outstanding balances are reconciled |
| Equity | Contributions; distributions or draws; other applicable equity accounts | Treatment appropriate to the practice’s ownership structure |

Keep account names stable and descriptive. For example, identify a bank account by its business purpose without placing sensitive account information in the name.

Assign responsibility for reviewing each balance-sheet group. If you handle routine entries and your accountant handles adjustments, document that division of work.

### Revenue and expense account groups

The income and expense side should reflect the services you provide, the products you sell, and the resources you use. The optometric practice guide *Best Practices in Financial Management* organizes financial discussion around categories including goods, staff, occupancy, and overhead. Those broad categories provide a useful starting structure. [Source: Best Practices in Financial Management](https://files.optometrybusiness.com/Best%20Practices%20of%20Financial%20Management.pdf)

| Account group | Suggested accounts | Practical boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Clinical revenue | Examination services; medical eye-care services; contact lens fitting services | Define service categories that do not overlap |
| Product revenue | Frames; spectacle lenses; contact lens products; accessories | Keep product sales distinguishable from professional services |
| Revenue adjustments | Returns; refunds; discounts; other approved adjustments | Define how each adjustment relates to the original activity |
| Product costs | Frame costs; spectacle lens or lab costs; contact lens costs; accessory costs | Coordinate posting with the inventory method |
| Doctor compensation | Employed doctor compensation; other accountant-approved categories | Keep compensation definitions distinct from owner distributions |
| Staff compensation | Staff wages; employer payroll taxes; benefits | Document which payroll components belong in each account |
| Occupancy | Rent; utilities; cleaning; security | Use a consistent facilities grouping |
| Technology | Practice software; other subscriptions; IT support | Define boundaries between software, support, and equipment |
| Administrative services | Bookkeeping; billing support; call support; other outside services | Categorize by the service purchased |
| Other operating expenses | Marketing; professional fees; insurance; office supplies; repairs | Add detail when it supports an actual review question |
| Accountant-managed expenses | Interest; depreciation; other applicable adjustments | Specify who posts and reviews these entries |

These are proposed account labels, not a requirement to create every account shown. Start with the categories you can support from your records. Add subdivisions when you have a clear reporting purpose and a reliable way to classify transactions.

## Keep Clinical Revenue and Optical Activity Distinct

### Map revenue according to the service or product

A useful distinction in eye-care reporting is the separation of clinical activity from optical activity. The American Academy of Ophthalmology’s AcadeMetrics survey distinguishes clinical collections, optical revenue, optical cost of goods sold, optician wages, and other optical expenses. [Source: AAO AcadeMetrics Survey Questions](https://www.aao.org/Assets/32e115ee-de89-4c50-9f9a-5965d6282aa9/637194422877430000/academetrics-surveyquestions-pdf)

Apply that distinction by creating a written mapping from your practice management system’s categories to your accounting categories. For example, consider separate destinations for contact lens fitting services and contact lens product sales.

Review bundled transactions with the person responsible for your accounting setup. Decide which supporting report will establish the allocation. Avoid assigning the entire transaction to whichever category seems most prominent.

Also define how revenue adjustments will be handled. Your bookkeeper should know where to record a product return or service-related adjustment and how to connect it to the appropriate category. Do not leave that decision to a different interpretation each month.

### Keep product costs and optical staffing identifiable

For optical reporting, decide where frame, lens, lab, and contact lens costs belong. Then establish a separate treatment for optician compensation and other optical expenses.

The AAO survey explicitly separates “Total Optical Cost of Goods Sold” from “Total Optician Gross Wages.” This distinction provides a useful model for keeping product costs and staff costs recognizable in your reports. [Source: AAO AcadeMetrics Survey Questions](https://www.aao.org/Assets/32e115ee-de89-4c50-9f9a-5965d6282aa9/637194422877430000/academetrics-surveyquestions-pdf)

For a practical account description, write both an inclusion and an exclusion:

> **Contact lens product costs:** Use for contact lens product costs under the practice’s approved inventory and cost-of-goods method. Exclude fitting-service expenses and staff compensation.

Treat that description as a proposed internal rule to confirm with your accountant. The account label alone does not establish when a purchase becomes an expense.

## Define Payroll, Occupancy, and Overhead Consistently

### Separate compensation categories with a clear purpose

Decide how you want to review doctor compensation, staff wages, payroll taxes, and benefits. If you need departmental reporting, determine whether your payroll records can support the proposed split.

A solo practice may prefer broad staff categories. A group may want additional reporting by department or location. In either case, write down how shared duties will be handled before allocating compensation across categories.

Avoid creating a detailed split that nobody can explain or maintain. If an employee works across several functions, use a documented approach agreed with your accountant and payroll administrator.

Keep owner compensation questions explicit. Ask your accountant to define the appropriate treatment of wages, draws, distributions, and contributions for your entity. Do not use a generic “owner pay” account as the default destination for every owner-related transaction.

### Give occupancy a consistent boundary

In its occupancy guidance, Review of Optometric Business includes rent, utilities, security, and cleaning among the costs to group together. It advises readers to “make sure all these categories fall under occupancy.” [Source: Review of Optometric Business](https://reviewob.com/key-occupancy-metrics)

You can use an occupancy parent category with separate accounts beneath it for the costs you want to inspect. That gives you a defined grouping while preserving useful detail.

Write down the boundary between facilities and equipment. A cleaning invoice and a clinical instrument repair may arrive from service providers, but they answer different expense questions. Classify them according to what was purchased.

If the practice owns its premises, have your accountant establish the relevant property, financing, and expense treatment. Do not automatically place every building-related payment into rent.

### Distinguish technology from outside administrative services

For [optometry practice operations](/industries/optometry/), it can be useful to distinguish software subscriptions, IT support, billing support, bookkeeping, and call support.

Choose categories based on the service received. A telephone platform subscription and an outside team answering calls may support the same workflow, but you may want to review their costs separately.

Where a vendor provides several services, inspect the invoice before creating an automatic posting rule. Use separate categories when the invoice provides a reasonable basis for doing so. If the package is not itemized, document the agreed treatment instead of inventing a detailed allocation.

Keep vendor names in the vendor records. Reserve account names for recurring types of activity so the chart remains understandable when suppliers change.

## Write Rules for Transactions That Need Judgment

### Review mixed invoices and uncertain classifications

A short account dictionary can carry much of the practical detail that does not belong in an account name. Include the account’s purpose, common examples, exclusions, and the person who resolves questions.

Use it to address recurring ambiguities:

| Transaction | Question to resolve |
|---|---|
| An invoice containing products and service charges | Can the components be identified and assigned separately? |
| A vendor credit | Which original category does the credit relate to? |
| A new equipment purchase | What treatment has the accountant approved? |
| A combined software and support package | Is separate reporting supported by the invoice or contract? |
| An unfamiliar transaction | Who will review it, and what documentation is needed? |

If your workflow uses a temporary review account, assign responsibility for resolving its entries. Give each unresolved item a specific question. “Confirm whether this is equipment or maintenance” is more useful than “Check later.”

### Coordinate inventory and tax-accounting decisions

The IRS *Tax Guide for Small Business* discusses business expenses, inventory, and cost of goods sold, including considerations relevant to businesses that provide services and sell products. It supplies tax-accounting context for the chart rather than an optometry-specific template. [Source: IRS Publication 334](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334)

Before changing product-cost accounts, ask your accountant to document how purchases, inventory balances, and cost of goods sold connect in your books. Confirm who posts inventory adjustments and which records support them.

Use the same approach for prepaid expenses, equipment, and financing transactions. Establish the accounting treatment first, then create or refine the account labels.

Keep the distinction between categorization and accounting method clear. Renaming an account does not resolve the underlying treatment of a transaction.

## Adapt the Structure for a Solo Office or a Group

### Keep a solo practice’s chart manageable

For a solo owner, every additional account creates another classification choice. Add detail where you expect to use it.

Before creating a subaccount, ask what you would do differently after reviewing it. If you routinely examine software spending, separate software from general office expenses. If a proposed subdivision has no clear use, keep the broader category until the need becomes concrete.

Review the proposed chart against actual invoice types and revenue reports. Work through ordinary transactions before adopting it. If several items fit equally well in different accounts, revise the descriptions.

The test is whether you and your bookkeeper would classify the same transaction consistently.

### Use shared definitions across locations

For a group, establish common definitions for revenue and expense accounts before comparing locations. “Clinical supplies,” “optical costs,” and “administrative services” should mean the same thing throughout the reporting structure.

Where your accounting system supports separate location or department tracking, consider using those fields alongside the account categories. Keep the type of activity and the place it occurred distinguishable.

Document how shared services will be reported. Specify which costs remain central, which are assigned to a location, and what supports any allocation.

If locations operate through separate entities, involve your accountant in designing entity-level records and consolidated reporting. A common account dictionary is useful preparation, but entity boundaries need their own accounting decisions.

## Put the Chart to Work in Your Monthly Review

### Map existing accounts before making changes

Begin with an export of the current chart and recent financial reports. Build a mapping worksheet showing each existing account, its proposed destination, the reason for the change, and any questions requiring review.

Identify duplicate labels, unclear categories, and accounts that mix unrelated activity. Review the mapping with your accountant before merging accounts or reclassifying historical transactions.

Choose a clear effective date and preserve the mapping. Decide how earlier periods will appear in comparison reports so a category change is not mistaken for a change in operations.

After implementation, work through representative transactions and inspect the resulting reports. Confirm that the entries appear where you intended.

### Read changes alongside the underlying activity

At each monthly review, inspect the revenue and expense categories that changed and ask what explains the movement. Consider transaction timing, account mapping, vendor credits, inventory entries, and changes in the services or products involved.

Optometric Management encourages owners to compare their practice with itself over time and to review expenses in relation to revenue. Use that approach to frame questions about your own reports. [Source: Optometric Management](https://optometricmanagement.com/issues/2010/november/use-metrics-in-your-practice)

Record the explanation and any follow-up responsibility. When a category repeatedly generates confusion, improve its definition before adding more accounts.

Your chart should remain a working tool: recognizable categories, documented boundaries, and a review routine you can maintain.
