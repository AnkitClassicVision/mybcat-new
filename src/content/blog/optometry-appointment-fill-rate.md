Appointment fill rate is the share of the slots your schedule offered that patients actually used. Across the 17 optometry practices in our fill rate reporting, it was 91.0% from Oct 24 to Nov 14, 2026, and the average practice still had about 13 open slots a week.

## What is appointment fill rate?

The math is one division:

**Fill rate = slots filled ÷ slots offered**

A no-show does not count as a filled slot. The slot was offered, but no visit took place, so it counts in the bottom number only.

A family medicine journal article on practice dashboards works out fill rate the same way, from filled appointments and the possible appointments in the schedule ([Family Practice Management](https://www.aafp.org/fpm/2023/1100/access-staffing-dashboard)).

The hard part is the bottom number. "Slots offered" should mean the slots your template actually opened for booking that week, for every provider and every appointment type. Decide up front how you treat blocked time, meetings and provider days off. If that rule changes from week to week, the rate moves even when nothing at the front desk changed.

An AHRQ handbook on practice facilitation warns that the wrong denominator can make any rate look better or worse than it is ([AHRQ](https://www.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/publications/files/practicefacilitationhandbook.pdf)).

A walk-in, or a cancelled slot that another patient took, counts as a filled slot, because a patient used the time. A same-day cancellation that nobody filled counts as an open slot.

Fill rate is not a no-show rate. It looks at the whole schedule, so it also catches time that was never booked. A practice can have few no-shows and still have a lot of empty time. For other access measures, see our [eye care KPI dashboard playbook](/blog/eye-care-kpi-dashboard-playbook/).

## What does our fill rate reporting show?

Across the 17 optometry practices in our fill rate reporting, 91.0% of appointment slots were filled over the last three tracked weeks, Oct 24 to Nov 14, 2026 (6,550 of 7,199). Eight weeks earlier, from Sept 19 to Oct 3, it was 88.9% (6,252 of 7,034).

| Period | Slots filled | Slots offered | Fill rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sept 19 to Oct 3 | 6,252 | 7,034 | 88.9% |
| Oct 24 to Nov 14, 2026 | 6,550 | 7,199 | 91.0% |

Even at 91.0% full, the average practice in our fill rate reporting still had about 13 open slots a week. A high percentage can still hide steady empty time.

Both periods were with MyBCAT, so the comparison shows where schedules ended up. It does not measure lift, the part of a change that came from our work. The last section explains more.

## What does the added-slots result show?

In our fill rate reporting, 5 practices added slots to their schedules. Together they added 431 slots, and they filled 422 more appointments. Where practices added slots, nearly all got filled.

What it shows: at those 5 practices, new time on the schedule did not sit empty. Filled appointments went up by almost as much as slots offered. For an owner thinking about another provider day or longer hours, that is a useful signal.

What it does not show: why the slots filled, or that added slots will fill at every practice. A group of 5 practices is small, and patient demand and provider mix differ from office to office. It does not say which appointment types filled, or whether some visits moved over from other days. It is not a forecast for your schedule.

## What does an open slot cost your practice?

Each open slot is a visit that could have been billed and was not. Here is a formula you can fill in with your own numbers:

**Revenue gap per year = open slots per week × net collected per visit × 48 weeks**

Open slots per week is slots offered minus slots filled, averaged over a few normal weeks. Net collected per visit is what the practice actually collects per visit after insurance adjustments and write-offs, across the exam and any optical sale that follows. Take it from your own reports, not from a published average. The 48 weeks stand for a working year after holidays and time off. Change it to match your own calendar.

Here is a worked example with letters instead of dollars. Say your schedule offered S slots last week and F of them were filled. Your open slots are O = S − F. If your practice nets N per visit, your yearly gap is O × N × 48. With about 13 open slots a week, like the average practice in our fill rate reporting, the gap is 13 × N × 48.

Now put in your own numbers: ____ open slots a week × ____ net collected per visit × 48 weeks = ____ a year.

Read the result as the size of the gap, not a target, since some open time absorbs urgent visits. Still, staff costs do not stop when a slot sits empty. A study of no-shows at a Veterans Affairs medical center notes that when a freed slot is not filled, the cost to pay the staff remains ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4714455/)). Another study lists underused resources, lower clinic efficiency and lower provider productivity among the effects of no-shows ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4187098/)).

The American Optometric Association describes the same problem for eye care. When patients do not show up or do not cancel, doctors have to close a gap of lost care and revenue. Its article lists reminders, cancellation fees, pre-appointing and overbooking among the ways practices respond ([AOA](https://www.aoa.org/news/practice-management/perfect-your-practice/3-solutions-for-noshow-patients)).

## How can you measure your fill rate this week?

You do not need new software to get a first number. You need one normal week of schedule data and a fixed way of counting it.

1. Pick a recent week with no holiday, and pull the schedule for every provider.
2. Count slots offered: every bookable slot your template opened, with blocked time handled by the same rule each week.
3. Count slots filled: slots where a patient was seen, walk-ins and refilled cancellations included.
4. Divide slots filled by slots offered for your fill rate. Subtract slots filled from slots offered for your open slots.
5. Repeat for a few more weeks so one slow week does not set the number, then put your average open slots into the formula above.

Write your counting rules down, so the next person counts the same way. If your system cannot report open slots directly, start with one provider and count from the exported schedule.

Once you have the number, look at who works the gaps. Filling an opening often means calling patients on a cancellation list or a recall list while the phones keep ringing. One optometrist writing in Review of Optometric Business describes keeping a cancellation list so the front desk can offer openings to patients who want an earlier visit ([Review of Optometric Business](https://reviewob.com/eliminate-empty-appointment-slots-turn-cancellations-into-cash/)). If that work falls on a desk that is already busy with check-in and phones, see how [front desk outsourcing](/solutions/front-desk-outsourcing/) works, and how [no-show recovery](/solutions/no-show-recovery/) follows up on missed visits.

## What do we not know yet?

We do not yet know how much of this we caused. Both periods in our fill rate reporting were with MyBCAT. So the numbers above show where schedules ended up. They do not compare those schedules with what would have happened without us.

Lift tracking, which compares each schedule on Monday morning with Friday close, started the week of Nov 23, 2026. That comparison shows the slots that were open at the start of a week and filled by its end. We will share the results when they exist.

Three tracked weeks is also a short window. A busy season or a slow one can move the rate, so read our numbers as a snapshot and your own numbers as the ones that count.
