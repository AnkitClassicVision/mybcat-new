For multi-location healthcare groups evaluating acquisition targets or [preparing a practice for sale](/optometry-practice-transitions/), the question of whether to engage a practice broker, and how to select one, carries significant operational and financial implications.

The optometry transaction landscape has evolved considerably, with private equity activity reshaping buyer expectations and valuation methodologies.

This guide examines the broker selection process through the lens of operations leaders managing portfolios of three or more locations, where transaction complexity and deal volume justify a more rigorous evaluation framework.

## What Does an Optometry Practice Broker Actually Do?

Understanding the scope of broker services helps operations teams assess where external expertise adds value versus where internal capabilities may suffice. The broker's role extends well beyond simply listing a practice for sale.

### Transaction Marketing and Buyer Sourcing

Practice brokers maintain networks of qualified buyers, including private equity firms, dental service organizations expanding into vision care, independent practitioners seeking ownership opportunities, and strategic acquirers looking to consolidate regional markets.

For sellers, this network access can meaningfully expand the pool of potential buyers beyond what organic outreach might achieve. The American Optometric Association recommends that practice owners consult with experienced transition professionals who maintain relationships with both buyers and sellers across multiple transaction types.

Brokers typically prepare confidential information memoranda, manage buyer inquiries to protect seller confidentiality during early stages, and coordinate site visits and management presentations.

For multi-location groups acquiring practices, understanding how brokers manage these processes helps set expectations for information access and timeline management.

### Valuation and Deal Structuring

Brokers often provide preliminary valuation opinions, though these should be distinguished from formal appraisals conducted by credentialed business valuators.

The broker's valuation perspective draws from recent transaction experience and market comparable data, which can inform negotiations but should not substitute for independent valuation work in material transactions.

Deal structure negotiation represents another core broker function. This includes purchase price allocation, earn-out provisions, seller financing terms, non-compete arrangements, and employment agreements for transitioning doctors.

The Medical Group Management Association emphasizes that legal considerations, compatibility assessment, and strategic planning all factor into successful practice acquisitions, areas where broker guidance can complement but not replace specialized legal and accounting counsel.

### Due Diligence Coordination

Once a letter of intent is signed, brokers typically coordinate the due diligence process, serving as an intermediary between buyer and seller professional teams. This includes managing document requests, scheduling access to financial records, and facilitating communication when issues arise.

Effective due diligence requires review of financial documents, compliance verification, and assembly of a professional team, responsibilities that experienced brokers help orchestrate but that ultimately require direct involvement from legal, accounting, and operational specialists.

## How Are Practice Broker Fees Typically Structured?

Fee structures vary considerably across the broker market, and understanding common arrangements helps operations teams evaluate total transaction costs and align broker incentives with organizational objectives.

### Commission-Based Models

The most common fee structure involves a success fee calculated as a percentage of the final transaction value. Commission rates typically range based on practice size, transaction complexity, and competitive market conditions.

Smaller single-location practices often command higher percentage commissions due to the fixed costs brokers incur regardless of deal size, while larger multi-practice transactions may negotiate more favorable rates.

Some brokers use tiered commission structures, where the percentage decreases as transaction value increases above certain thresholds. Others apply minimum fee floors to ensure adequate compensation for smaller transactions.

For acquirers making multiple purchases, negotiating volume-based fee arrangements or retainer structures can reduce per-transaction costs while ensuring consistent broker engagement.

### Retainer and Hybrid Arrangements

Retainer-based engagements require upfront payments, typically applied against eventual success fees upon transaction close. These arrangements demonstrate seller commitment, which can improve broker attention and marketing effort, while providing brokers with compensation for preliminary work even if transactions do not close.

Hybrid models combine modest retainers with reduced success fees, balancing risk between broker and client. For multi-location groups with ongoing acquisition programs, annual retainer arrangements with per-deal success fees can provide preferred access to deal flow while managing total costs.

### Buyer-Side Representation

Most practice brokers represent sellers, but some offer buyer-side representation services. Buyer-side brokers help acquirers identify suitable targets, conduct preliminary screening, and negotiate on the buyer's behalf.

Fee structures for buyer representation vary, sometimes involving retainers, success fees based on purchase price, or flat fees per completed transaction. Groups with active acquisition strategies should clarify whether engaging a broker creates any conflicts with that broker's seller representation activities.

## What Criteria Matter Most When Evaluating Brokers?

Not all practice brokers bring equivalent capabilities, and the evaluation criteria that matter most depend on transaction specifics and organizational priorities.

### Optometry-Specific Transaction Experience

Healthcare practice transactions involve regulatory, licensing, and operational considerations that differ meaningfully from general business sales.

Within healthcare, optometry practices present distinct characteristics, optical retail components, vision plan reimbursement dynamics, equipment considerations, and professional licensing requirements, that benefit from specialized experience.

The American Optometric Association recommends consulting with doctors who have completed successful transactions when evaluating professional advisors. Asking broker candidates for references from completed optometry transactions, and specifically contacting those references, provides insight into how brokers have handled specialty-specific challenges.

Review of Optometric Business and similar trade publications cover practice transition topics including broker selection considerations that can inform evaluation criteria.

### Geographic Market Knowledge

Local market dynamics significantly influence practice valuations and buyer interest. Brokers with deep experience in specific geographic markets understand local competitive conditions, referral patterns, reimbursement environments, and buyer pools.

For multi-location groups operating across diverse markets, evaluating whether a broker's geographic expertise aligns with transaction locations matters more than national reach alone.

Brokers should demonstrate familiarity with regional private equity activity, competing buyer groups, and local practitioner networks. Market knowledge manifests in realistic valuation expectations, efficient buyer targeting, and effective navigation of local professional relationships.

### Transaction Volume and Success Rates

Understanding a broker's transaction volume and close rates helps assess execution capability. However, these metrics require context, brokers with highly selective engagement criteria may show higher close rates but may not accept smaller or more complex transactions.

Conversely, high volume brokers may demonstrate market presence but could spread attention across too many concurrent engagements.

Asking about current engagement levels, typical time to close, and how the broker manages multiple concurrent transactions reveals capacity constraints that could affect attention to any single deal.

## When Does Hiring a Broker Make Strategic Sense?

The decision to engage a broker involves weighing transaction costs against execution risk and internal capability limitations. Different scenarios favor different approaches.

### Scenarios Favoring Broker Engagement

First-time sellers typically benefit from broker guidance through unfamiliar processes. Without transaction experience, sellers may undervalue practices, make avoidable negotiation mistakes, or fail to prepare adequately for buyer due diligence.

The AOA notes that practice transitions benefit from consultation with experienced professionals who understand both buyer and seller perspectives.

Sellers seeking confidentiality during early marketing stages often find broker intermediation valuable. Brokers can market practices to qualified buyers without immediately revealing seller identity, protecting against employee concerns, patient anxiety, and competitive intelligence leakage.

Managing this confidentiality internally proves difficult, particularly for practices with high staff or patient sensitivity.

Complex transactions involving multiple locations, partnership structures, earnout negotiations, or private equity buyers often justify broker involvement. MGMA guidance on private equity acquisitions emphasizes the importance of professional support when navigating these transaction types, which carry different risk profiles than traditional practice sales.

### Factors Suggesting Internal Execution

Multi-location groups with established acquisition programs may develop sufficient internal transaction expertise to reduce broker dependency. When operations teams include members with M&A experience, when legal counsel has completed similar transactions, and when buyer relationships already exist, the incremental value of broker engagement diminishes.

Direct relationship transactions, where buyer and seller already know each other, may not require broker marketing services.

Associate-to-owner transitions, mergers between friendly competitors, or sales to known strategic acquirers may proceed efficiently without intermediary involvement, though professional advisors for valuation, legal, and tax matters remain important.

Situations involving limited buyer interest, perhaps due to location, practice condition, or market conditions, may not justify broker fees. If realistic assessment suggests a narrow buyer pool that internal efforts can reach directly, broker commissions may not generate proportional value.

## What Questions Should You Ask During Broker Interviews?

Structured broker interviews help operations teams compare candidates consistently and surface relevant experience, capabilities, and potential concerns.

Sellers who need the broader sequence from preparation through diligence and closing can use this [guide to selling an optometry practice](/blog/guide-for-selling-your-optometry-practice/) alongside the broker-selection questions below.

### Experience and Track Record Questions

Request specific examples of comparable transactions, practices of similar size, in similar markets, with similar buyer types. Ask how those transactions progressed, what challenges arose, and how outcomes compared to initial expectations.

MGMA guidance on M&A processes emphasizes assessing compatibility and strategic fit, principles that apply equally to selecting professional advisors.

Inquire about transactions that did not close and understand why. Brokers who acknowledge unsuccessful engagements and explain contributing factors demonstrate self-awareness that informs realistic expectation-setting. Brokers who claim universal success may be overstating results or only accepting highly certain transactions.

Ask about the broker's professional background, including prior practice ownership, healthcare administration experience, or financial services credentials. Understanding what shaped the broker's expertise helps evaluate fit for specific transaction needs.

### Process and Communication Questions

Clarify how the broker will communicate throughout the engagement, reporting frequency, key milestone notifications, and escalation procedures for issues. Multi-location groups managing multiple concurrent transactions need predictable communication cadences.

Understand the broker's approach to buyer qualification, how they screen inquiries, assess buyer financial capability, and evaluate strategic fit. Ineffective qualification wastes seller time on uncommitted or incapable buyers.

Ask about the broker's support team and who specifically will handle various engagement phases. Senior brokers may secure engagements but delegate execution to junior staff; understanding the actual working team clarifies what expertise will be applied directly.

### Fee and Engagement Questions

Request detailed fee structure explanations in writing, including all potential charges beyond success fees. Marketing expenses, travel costs, third-party services, and other pass-through charges can add materially to total transaction costs.

Clarify engagement exclusivity terms, duration, and termination provisions. Understand what happens if the transaction does not close, whether any fees are owed, how proprietary materials are handled, and what restrictions apply to subsequent broker engagements.

For multi-location groups, explore volume arrangements, preferred terms for repeat engagements, or retainer structures that align with ongoing transaction programs.

## How Can Multi-Location Groups Optimize Their Broker Relationships?

Operations leaders at healthcare groups with three or more locations face distinct considerations when engaging practice brokers, given transaction volume, portfolio complexity, and strategic positioning.

### Building Institutional Knowledge

Rather than treating each transaction as isolated, document broker performance across engagements. Track metrics including time to close, final price versus initial valuation, buyer quality, and issue resolution effectiveness. This institutional memory informs future broker selection and negotiation use.

Capture lessons learned from each transaction, what broker actions added value, where internal teams could have contributed more, and how engagement terms could improve.

Medical Economics notes that acquisition checklists covering due diligence steps and valuation considerations help organize transaction knowledge, which extends to managing broker relationships systematically.

### Developing Internal Capabilities

Multi-location groups completing multiple acquisitions annually may justify building internal transaction capabilities that reduce broker dependency over time. This might include dedicated corporate development staff, transaction-experienced legal counsel, and standardized due diligence processes.

Internal capability development does not necessarily eliminate broker value but changes the nature of engagement. Groups with strong internal teams may engage brokers primarily for deal sourcing and confidential marketing while handling negotiation and due diligence coordination internally.

### Aligning Broker Incentives

Consider how fee structures align broker incentives with organizational objectives. Standard percentage-of-price commissions may not encourage brokers to prioritize deal terms that matter to buyers, such as favorable earnout structures or transition support commitments.

Creative fee arrangements might include bonuses for achieving specific deal terms, penalties for material post-close issues traceable to broker representations, or performance holdbacks released based on transition success metrics.

Not all brokers will accept modified structures, but sophisticated brokers serving institutional clients often accommodate customized arrangements.
