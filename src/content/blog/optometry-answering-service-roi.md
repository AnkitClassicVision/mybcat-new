An answering service earns its place in a multi-location optometry group when it improves access without creating a second, ungoverned front desk. The business case is not a generic comparison of an hourly rate to a vendor invoice. It is a comparison of the current cost to answer, schedule, document, and recover patient demand across the network against the fully loaded cost of a managed, measurable access model.

For a group with three or more locations, the missed opportunity is often hidden in the gaps between sites: a call that reaches voicemail during a busy check-in period, an appointment request that waits for a callback, a scheduling rule known only to one office, or an after-hours message that lacks a clear owner. A centralized service can address those gaps, but only if leadership defines what the team may do, how it records an outcome, and where it must escalate. The broader [front desk outsourcing model](/solutions/front-desk-outsourcing/) explains the operating layer behind that work.

This article gives optometry operators a practical way to calculate ROI, establish a credible baseline, and decide whether a pilot deserves expansion. It does not assume that every call should leave the office or that lower labor cost automatically produces value.

## How should a multi-location optometry group calculate answering service ROI?

Begin with a 30-day baseline by location, daypart, and call type. A network average is not enough. It can hide an office that answers almost every call behind one that consistently sends callers to voicemail. Capture incoming calls, answered calls, abandoned calls, callback completion, appointment requests, scheduled appointments, reschedules, and messages that return to the office for rework. Pair those measures with staffing, overtime, temporary coverage, management time, and current vendor spend.

The resulting calculation should distinguish between value that is observed and value that is assumed. A useful monthly model is:

> **Net monthly value = documented incremental contribution from completed access outcomes + avoidable operating cost removed − managed-service investment − transition and oversight cost.**

“Documented incremental contribution” is deliberately narrower than the value of every answered call. A group should only credit an answering service for appointments or retained demand that its baseline suggests would otherwise have been lost, delayed, or handled with additional cost. The relevant contribution amount is the group’s own approved measure, such as contribution per completed visit or contribution per scheduled eligible appointment. Finance should own the definition and avoid substituting gross charges for contribution.

The second component is operating cost. Include more than wages: benefits, payroll taxes, overtime, agency coverage, recruiting, onboarding, supervisor time, and the cost of duplicated phone coverage across sites. Do not assume that all on-site positions disappear after centralization. In-office teams still own arrivals, departures, face-to-face support, provider coordination, and exceptions needing site context. The question is whether repeatable inbound work is consuming capacity that can be handled reliably in a shared queue.

Use the same calculation for each location and the network total. That makes an ROI review useful as an operating tool, not merely a vendor justification. For a complementary financial framework, see [how optometry groups can evaluate a BPO cost-benefit case](/blog/conducting-a-cost-benefit-analysis-of-bpo-in-optometry/).

## What should count as recovered value from missed calls?

Not every missed call is a lost patient, and treating it that way will make the business case fragile. Start by separating calls into categories: new appointment requests, existing-patient scheduling, reschedules, routine administrative questions, billing questions, clinical concerns, and wrong numbers or spam. Then identify the categories the access team is authorized to resolve and the downstream outcome that shows resolution.

For appointment requests, recovery should be tied to a completed scheduling action, not just a conversation. For existing patients, it may be a completed reschedule, a confirmed callback, or a routed request closed by the named owner. A [missed-call revenue framework for multi-location healthcare](/blog/missed-calls-revenue-leak-multi-location-healthcare/) can help leadership connect demand loss to a measurable process without inflating the result.

This is also where location-level rules matter. One office may have provider-specific templates, an optical service constraint, or a referral workflow that requires an exception. A central team needs that rule in the approved knowledge base, not in an individual employee’s memory. Otherwise, the group may improve answer rate while producing transfers, incorrect bookings, or a higher cancellation burden.

Do not assign clinical judgment to a non-clinical answering team. Its role is to follow approved administrative scripts, collect approved information, and route time-sensitive or clinical matters through the group’s designated escalation path. That boundary protects the patient experience and prevents an ROI project from creating a safety problem.

## How do distributed front-desk costs compare with centralized coverage?

The comparison is between operating models, not between people. Distributed coverage can be appropriate when a location needs continuous on-site coordination. It becomes expensive when every site carries overlapping phone capacity, absorbs volume peaks independently, and retrains staff on the same intake work after turnover.

Build a fully loaded cost view for the current model. For every location, include the portion of front-desk time spent on calls, callbacks, schedule changes, message intake, and related documentation. Add overtime, short-term coverage, recruiting and training, supervisor intervention, and the cost of work that spills into clinical or management time. Then map the same work into a proposed centralized model: which calls remain at the site, which flow to the queue, what system access is required, and who owns exceptions.

Centralization can create value through pooled coverage, a shared training program, standard reporting, and less variation in routine work. It also has real costs: implementation effort, integration work, ongoing QA, governance meetings, and retained on-site coverage. A credible model includes all of them. MGMA’s discussion of [centralized scheduling](https://www.mgma.com/articles/implementing-central-scheduling-to-support-practice-growth-and-success-in-the-accountable-care-environment) is a useful external reference for examining scheduling as a coordinated operating function rather than a set of isolated desks.

The best comparison has three cases: retain the current model, add overflow and after-hours coverage, or move defined call types into centralized coverage. This lets leadership compare a limited pilot with broader redesign without claiming savings that depend on an unapproved staffing change.

## Which KPIs prove that the service is improving patient access?

An ROI dashboard should connect access performance to completed work. Answer rate is useful, but it cannot prove that callers received the right outcome. Report the following measures by location, queue, daypart, and call type:

- answer rate and abandonment rate, with a clear definition of when a call is considered abandoned;
- average speed to answer and callback completion within the group’s stated service level;
- appointment request-to-scheduled rate for calls the team is authorized to schedule;
- transfer rate, repeat-contact rate, and the reason a call returns to an office;
- appointment outcomes, such as completed reschedules or scheduled appointments that later cancel;
- quality-review results for identity verification, approved scheduling rules, documentation, and escalation use; and
- fully loaded cost per resolved access outcome.

These measures should be reviewed alongside each other. A lower handle time is not an improvement if transfers or repeat calls rise. A higher scheduling rate is not a win if incorrect appointment types create downstream rework. The relevant question for an executive team is whether the group is getting more completed, correct access outcomes for each dollar it spends.

Groups can add the measures to a [multi-location intake KPI dashboard](/blog/kpi-dashboard-multi-location-intake/) and compare them with the wider [patient access center metrics used by healthcare executives](/blog/patient-access-center-metrics-healthcare-executives/). Those views make site variation visible without reducing performance management to one network average.

## What call patterns make a centralized model more valuable?

Centralized coverage is most useful when demand is uneven. Look for high-volume windows that interrupt check-in and check-out, recurring lunch or late-day gaps, after-hours requests, seasonal scheduling demand, and locations where one absence materially changes answer performance. The objective is not to force every call through a shared queue. It is to use shared capacity where it prevents a predictable access failure.

Review the pattern by hour and by weekday before selecting coverage hours. A group spread across time zones may be able to balance volume between locations, but only if routing recognizes each site’s time zone, provider availability, and approved appointment rules. A group concentrated in one region may need extra capacity during synchronized peaks. Both models need a documented routing plan and a way to audit what happened.

After-hours coverage deserves its own workflow. Routine scheduling questions, messages for the next business day, and urgent clinical concerns must not receive the same treatment. Define what the answering team may schedule or record, what requires an immediate handoff, and what the site team must see at opening. ## How should an acquired or PE-backed group evaluate the model?

Acquisitions often expose the exact variation that makes a centralized access model worth evaluating: different phone trees, different scheduling templates, different documentation habits, and different expectations for callbacks. The goal is not to erase every site distinction on day one. It is to establish one accountable method for routine work while preserving approved location and provider rules.

For an acquired group, measure the current state before changing it. Identify the call categories, routing logic, system access, templates, escalation contacts, and reporting definitions in use at each site. Then define the first common workflow, usually a limited set of routine appointment or message-intake calls. A pilot that makes those differences visible is more valuable than a fast cutover that buries them.

Private equity sponsors and operating teams should treat the ROI model as evidence for an integration decision, not a valuation promise. A central service can support consistent reporting and fewer duplicated processes, but enterprise value depends on many factors outside call handling. Harvard Business Review’s [private equity topic collection](https://hbr.org/topic/private-equity) is a relevant source for broader diligence and operating-model context. The internal [90-day healthcare acquisition integration playbook](/blog/90-day-integration-playbook-healthcare-acquisitions/) shows how patient-access work can be staged alongside the wider integration effort.

## How should leaders stage rollout without distorting the ROI result?

Start with a bounded workflow and a small group of locations that represent the real operating environment. A suitable first scope might cover overflow appointment requests and approved after-hours administrative messages. It should include the same systems, scripts, escalation paths, QA criteria, and reporting that a broader rollout would require. Do not call a pilot successful because a project manager manually repairs every failure.

Before launch, document the baseline, source system, data definitions, service levels, and decision owner for each metric. During the pilot, review call samples and exceptions every week. Separate issues caused by training, routing, knowledge-base gaps, scheduling configuration, and legitimate site-specific rules. Each change should have an owner and a record, so the program does not collect informal workarounds.

After the first stable period, compare the pilot with its own baseline and a comparable non-pilot location where possible. Look for improved completion of authorized work, lower rework, and consistent quality. Then decide whether to expand the scope, correct the workflow, or stop. That sequence makes the return credible because it treats implementation effort as part of the investment.

## What implementation mistakes usually erase the expected return?

The first mistake is buying coverage before defining the work. If scripts, scheduling permissions, escalation rules, and documentation standards are unclear, a service will either transfer too much work back to the sites or make inconsistent decisions. Both outcomes weaken the patient experience and make financial results difficult to interpret.

The second is measuring activity instead of outcomes. High call volume, fast answers, and long agent hours can coexist with poor booking quality or a growing callback backlog. Tie the scorecard to resolved access outcomes and review a sample of interactions against the rules the group approved.

The third is treating integration and access control as implementation details. The team needs role-appropriate system access, a controlled knowledge base, structured dispositions, and a clear way to record an unresolved request. If a service handles protected health information, the group should involve its compliance and legal stakeholders in the applicable business associate and security review before operations begin. The [enterprise healthcare patient-access compliance guide](/blog/enterprise-healthcare-patient-access-compliance-hipaa-soc2/) covers the operational controls that belong beside the ROI model.

Finally, do not judge the program only during its learning period or let it run without calibration. Early performance will expose real gaps in routing, training, and local data. Ongoing QA, change control, and location-level reporting are what turn a coverage arrangement into a managed access function.
