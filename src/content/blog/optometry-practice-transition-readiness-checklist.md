An optometry practice transition readiness checklist is a neutral, owner-facing way to put current observations, personal priorities, and open questions in one place. It is not a scorecard.

It does not need to produce a conclusion. Its purpose is simpler: help you describe the practice clearly enough to see what you understand and what you want to consider further.

The American Optometric Association describes succession planning as a proactive subject that reaches beyond a single event.

Its discussion includes the practice, patients, team, successor, community continuity, and the owner's legacy.

That broad perspective gives an owner a useful reason to pause and make notes before trying to answer larger questions.

Use this checklist as a working document. Date your notes, write in plain language, and separate a direct observation from a personal preference. You can return to it as your thinking changes.

## Begin With Owner Priorities

The AOA's discussion of practice transitions describes planning as a multi-year topic shaped in part by owner goals and a desired future role.

Begin there. A short page about what matters to you creates context for every other section of the checklist.

### Name What Matters in Your Work

Write a few sentences about the professional work that matters most to you today.

You might describe the responsibilities you enjoy, the qualities you associate with the practice, or the relationships that give your work meaning.

The point is not to write a polished statement. It is to record your current view accurately.

Try prompts such as these:

- What parts of my work feel most important to me?
- Which responsibilities do I want to describe more clearly?
- What qualities do I associate with this practice?
- Which question about the future keeps returning?

Keep a preference separate from an observation. “I value a relationship-centered practice” is a preference.

“The practice serves a particular local area” is an observation. Both may matter, but clear labels help you read your own notes later.

### Describe the Future Role You Want to Consider

The AOA identifies an owner's desired future role as one consideration in multi-year planning.

Use that idea as a prompt rather than a commitment. Describe the role you can imagine for yourself, then note what remains uncertain.

You may want to consider how much of your time you want connected with clinical work, leadership, mentoring, or other responsibilities.

You do not need to settle every point at once. A useful note can be as modest as, “I want to think more about this role,” followed by a specific question.

### Keep a Dated Record of Changes

Owner priorities can change. Rather than treating an early note as final, date it and revise it when your view evolves.

A dated record makes the movement in your thinking visible without requiring you to defend an older answer.

At each review, ask: What still feels accurate? What has changed? What is the smallest question I want to consider next? Those questions keep the document focused on clarity.

## Describe the Practice Today

The AOA names location, competition, leases, equipment, supplies, growth, staff knowledge, patient relationships, professional philosophy, and transition arrangements among practice factors to evaluate.

Use those topics as a neutral framework for observation.

For each topic, add three short fields: “What I know,” “What I want to understand,” and “My next question.” This format gives uncertainty a visible place.

It is better to write that a detail needs attention than to turn an assumption into a fact.

### Note Location and Competition

Begin with a plain-language description of the location. Record the setting, the surrounding area, and the features you associate with the practice's daily character.

Stay descriptive. The goal is not to forecast what the setting may mean; it is to state what you observe now.

Create a separate note for competition, another AOA practice factor. Record what you notice in the local professional setting and which parts of that picture you want to understand better. Useful prompts include:

- How would I describe the location to someone unfamiliar with the practice?
- What features of the surrounding area stand out?
- What observations describe the competitive setting?
- Which notes are clear, and which need a closer look?

### Give Lease Notes Their Own Page

Leases appear in the AOA's list of practice factors. Keep lease notes in their own section rather than blending them into general location comments.

Write down the documents you recognize, the terms you understand, and the terms that lead to a question.

This is an exercise in owner clarity, not interpretation.

A note such as “I recognize this document and want to understand this section better” is useful because it identifies the precise point that needs attention. If a question feels broad, rewrite it in smaller words.

### List Equipment and Supplies by Category

Equipment and supplies are also named by the AOA. A simple category list can help you describe them without trying to capture every item at once.

Start with groups that make sense in the practice. For each group, note its role, the records you recognize, and one question you want to revisit.

Keep these entries descriptive. They can state what is present and where you would look for more context. They do not need numbers, rankings, or conclusions.

A short list with clear headings is easier to review than an exhaustive list that leaves the owner unsure what matters.

## Observe Growth and Team Knowledge

The AOA includes growth and staff knowledge in its practice-factor list. In a readiness checklist, treat both as current observations.

Write how you describe the practice's direction today, then identify the knowledge connected with different roles and routines.

### Write About Growth Without a Forecast

Use ordinary language to describe the practice's current direction.

You might note changes you have observed, areas that receive more attention, or questions that have emerged over time. Do not turn a short observation into a prediction.

The useful readiness question is whether you can describe the present picture clearly. If you cannot, write down the one detail you would like to understand first.

Small, concrete questions are easier to return to than a broad request for every answer at once.

### Map Knowledge to Routines

Staff knowledge can be recorded without evaluating people. Name a routine, the role that is most familiar with it, and the question you want to consider.

The purpose is to see where knowledge appears to sit within the practice and where your own picture is incomplete.

For example, you might list a routine related to scheduling, optical work, or office operations.

Then write what you understand about that routine and what you want to ask next. Keep the note focused on the routine, not on a judgment about the person connected with it.

### Separate Observation From Interpretation

An observation says what you notice. An interpretation adds meaning. Both can have a place in an owner document, but they should not be confused.

“This role is closely connected with this routine” is an observation. “This means the practice should do something” is an interpretation.

When you label the difference, you create room to think without forcing an immediate answer.

This is especially useful when several practice factors seem connected and you are still deciding what question belongs first.

## Reflect on Relationships and Philosophy

Patient relationships and professional philosophy are included in the AOA's practice-factor list. These subjects are well suited to owner reflection.

Describe the relationship-centered qualities you associate with the practice and the professional principles that guide your work.

### Use First-Person Language for Values

First-person language makes it clear when a note reflects your own view.

You might write, “I want the practice to be known for…” or “The professional principle that matters most to me is…” These statements are not predictions. They are a record of what you value now.

If a phrase feels vague, replace it with a simple example from your experience. Specific language can make a reflection easier to recognize on a later review.

Keep the example focused on your perspective rather than presenting it as a universal rule.

### Describe Patient Relationships Carefully

Write about the qualities you associate with patient relationships in the practice. You may note the habits, standards, or identity that you believe shape those relationships.

The goal is to make your perspective legible to yourself, not to make a broad claim about every experience.

Questions can help: Which relationship-centered qualities matter most to me? What words best describe the professional approach I want to preserve in my thinking? Which reflection is clear, and which one needs simpler language?

### Connect Philosophy to Daily Work

Professional philosophy can feel abstract until you connect it with the daily work you recognize.

List a principle, then add a short note about where you see it reflected. If the connection is uncertain, write that uncertainty down.

This section is not meant to settle a final account of the practice. It simply helps an owner identify the principles that remain important while considering the future role and the current practice picture.

## Create an Open-Questions Page

After making your first notes, create one page titled “Open questions.” Move unanswered questions there and add a date beside each one.

This gives a clear boundary between what you can describe today and what you want to consider further.

### Sort Questions by Practice Factor

Use headings that match the checklist: owner priorities, future role, location, competition, leases, equipment, supplies, growth, staff knowledge, patient relationships, and professional philosophy.

A question can appear under more than one heading if that makes it easier to find.

The document is for your clarity, so it does not need a perfect filing system. It needs a structure you can use. Keep each question short enough that you can tell what it is asking without rereading several paragraphs.

### Give Every Question a Next Step

Add one neutral label to each question:

- I can answer this from my own understanding.
- I want to review an existing note.
- I want another perspective on this subject.
- I need to narrow this question first.

These labels are prompts, not instructions. They prevent a broad subject from becoming an unstructured list.

They also make it easier to see whether the next useful action is reflection, review, or a more precise question.

### Review the Checklist Over Time

The AOA describes transition planning as a multi-year topic shaped by owner goals and a desired future role. That supports using this checklist as a working document rather than a final statement.

At a review, ask three questions: What do I understand more clearly now? Which notes no longer describe the practice accurately? What is the next smallest question I want to consider? A brief review can keep the checklist readable and useful.

## Use the Checklist as a Neutral Record

A readiness checklist works when you can pick it up and understand it. Use a consistent date format, keep notes brief, and separate direct observations from personal reflections.

A notebook, document, or simple three-column page can work. The format matters less than whether it remains clear to you.

### Keep the Scope Modest

This checklist is not designed to answer every question about an optometry practice transition. It is a place to record owner priorities, a desired future role, current practice factors, and open questions.

That modest scope is useful because it helps you see the next question without pretending to resolve the whole topic.

### End Each Review With One Question

Choose one question that deserves attention next.

It may concern a lease note, an equipment category, a routine, a reflection about patient relationships, or your own future role. Write it in direct language and date it.

The checklist succeeds when it gives you a clearer record of what you know, what remains open, and which question you want to consider next.
