Bookkeeping for optometry practices starts with a practical question: can you explain the transactions in your books using the records behind them? For an owner handling the finances between appointments, that means having a consistent way to organize receipts, classify activity, reconcile accounts, and review unresolved items.

The American Optometric Association describes financial management responsibilities that include banking arrangements and “handling the monies from your practice.” Bookkeeping provides the transaction records for that broader responsibility.

[American Optometric Association: Managing Practice Finances](https://www.aoa.org/practice/owning-a-practice/managing-practice-finances).

Whether you run a solo office or a group, build the routine around your actual work: professional services, optical sales, contact lenses, supplier bills, payroll, and collections.

Treat it as a defined part of your [optometry practice operations](/industries/optometry/), with named responsibilities and a regular review.

## Establish a Clear Foundation for Your Books

### Keep business accounts separate

Begin with dedicated business banking and a clear record of which accounts belong in the practice’s books.

IRS Publication 583 discusses separating business and personal accounts, recording transactions, and reconciling the business checking account. [IRS Publication 583](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p583.pdf).

Create an account register that identifies each business bank account, credit card, and payment processor, along with the person responsible for obtaining its statements.

Use account nicknames in routine checklists rather than copying sensitive account details.

When you contribute personal funds or pay a practice expense personally, flag the transaction for the bookkeeper or accountant.

Ask for a documented approach to owner contributions, withdrawals, and reimbursements. Apply that approach consistently instead of choosing a category based on which option looks closest in the software.

Also confirm whether your books use a cash or accrual basis. Have your accountant document how that choice applies to the reports and entries you use.

### Organize supporting documents around transactions

The IRS identifies supporting records including “sales slips, paid bills, invoices, receipts, deposit slips.” Its guidance covers documentation for income, purchases, expenses, assets, and employment taxes.

[IRS recordkeeping guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/what-kind-of-records-should-i-keep).

For your workflow, establish a designated location for business documents, organized by accounting period and document type. Give supplier invoices a consistent naming convention using the vendor, invoice date, and invoice reference.

Create a separate queue for missing documentation. Each unresolved item should have a description, an assigned person, and a next action.

An unfamiliar transaction should remain a question until someone provides the evidence needed to classify it.

Keep patient details out of general bookkeeping notes and shared task lists. Use aggregate reports for routine financial review, with any necessary detailed investigation handled within the practice’s approved systems.

## Build an Optometry Chart of Accounts

### Separate the activities you want to understand

A chart of accounts is the category structure used to organize your books.

Review of Optometric Business presents an optometry example that separates service, optical, and contact lens income and addresses cost of goods sold, payroll, and other expenses.

[Review of Optometric Business: Chart of Accounts](https://staging.reviewob.com/chart-of-accounts-how-to-create-this-chart-to-better-measure-and-improve-your-profitability).

Use that structure as a starting point for a conversation with your accountant. The following is a planning outline, rather than a prescribed accounting treatment:

| Area | Categories to discuss | Question for the owner |
|---|---|---|
| Professional services | Service income | Which service distinctions will you actually review? |
| Optical sales | Frames and spectacle lenses | Do you want separate views of these activities? |
| Contact lenses | Contact lens income | How will you distinguish products from professional services? |
| Product costs | Frames, lenses, contacts, and lab fees | How should purchases and inventory flow through the books? |
| Staffing | Payroll and related expense categories | Which supporting reports will explain these entries? |
| Practice operations | Occupancy, software, supplies, and professional services | Which categories need separate attention? |

Choose categories that answer recurring management questions. Before adding another account, identify the report or decision that requires it.

### Write down the coding rules

Create a short coding guide alongside the chart of accounts. For each frequently used category, describe what belongs there, what supporting document to consult, and which questions require accountant review.

Include instructions for ambiguous transactions. A supplier invoice might contain different types of purchases; a familiar vendor name alone should not determine the category. Read the invoice and apply the agreed rules.

For a group, document how locations are identified and how shared expenses are assigned. Ask your accountant to approve the approach to shared costs and transactions between entities. Keep those instructions distinct from ordinary office coding.

Record changes to the guide with an effective date. When a category changes, ask how comparisons with earlier periods should be presented.

## Connect Collections With Bank Deposits

### Trace deposits through the supporting reports

Build a repeatable path from your collection summaries to settlement records and bank activity. Define which report supplies each bookkeeping entry, and ask your accountant to review the mapping before you rely on it.

For each deposit under review, work through these questions:

- Which collection or settlement report supports it?
- What period does that report cover?
- Does the settlement documentation show fees, refunds, or adjustments?
- Has the related activity already entered the accounting system through an import?
- What remains unexplained?

If a settlement crosses reporting periods, document the dates involved and ask how to handle it. If an import and a bank feed both reference the same activity, establish which entry should be matched rather than added again.

Do not create an unsupported adjustment simply to make the reconciliation agree. Record the discrepancy, identify the source records needed, and assign the investigation. Close the item only when the explanation supports the treatment.

### Give receivables a separate review

Bank reconciliation and accounts receivable review deserve separate places in your routine.

Optometric Management’s “Closing the Year Strong” discusses aging reports, overdue balances, write-offs, and revenue recognition as part of an optometry receivables review.

[Optometric Management: Closing the Year Strong](https://digital.optometricmanagement.com/articles/closing-the-year-strong).

Start the owner’s review with an aggregate aging report. Ask the billing lead to explain unresolved categories and distinguish items awaiting follow-up from items needing an accounting decision.

Use specific questions: Is a payment awaiting posting? Is a balance disputed? Is an adjustment awaiting authorization? Does the accountant need to review its treatment?

Define who can recommend an adjustment and who approves it. Keep the approval and accounting instructions with the appropriate supporting records. Avoid clearing an old balance solely because it has appeared on several reports.

For the bookkeeping handoff, request a concise summary of changes and outstanding questions without copying patient information into general financial notes.

## Organize Expenses and Optical Inventory

### Follow bills from receipt through payment

Choose a standard route for incoming supplier invoices. Identify who receives the document, confirms the purchase, approves payment, and records it. In a solo practice, you may perform each task yourself; still record when each step is complete.

Before paying a bill, check its invoice reference against the records already entered. Review any related credit memo, return, or disputed charge.

If the invoice does not match your understanding of the purchase, resolve the question with the supplier before treating it as complete.

Maintain an upcoming-payment list using approved business records. Include the due date, approval status, and any unresolved issue. Review recurring charges against the current service agreement or invoice.

For payroll entries, use the provider’s supporting reports and have your accountant establish the mapping.

Keep equipment purchases and financing documents available for accounting review instead of assigning treatment from the bank description alone.

### Agree on an inventory process with your accountant

The optometry-specific *Best Practices of Financial Management* guide includes sample financial statements with categories for frames, lenses, contacts, and lab fees.

It also discusses inventory and cost-of-goods accounting. [Best Practices of Financial Management](https://files.optometrybusiness.com/Best%20Practices%20of%20Financial%20Management.pdf).

Translate that subject into a written operating process for your practice. Ask your accountant which inventory records are needed, how purchases should be recorded, and what information supports period-end adjustments.

Assign responsibility for recording product receipts, supplier returns, credits, and stock movements. Where your records show a difference between physical stock and recorded stock, investigate the supporting activity before making an adjustment.

For multiple offices, document transfers between locations using consistent product references and receiving confirmation. Keep transfers distinguishable from purchases and sales in the operational records.

Finish each inventory review with a list of resolved discrepancies and items awaiting a decision. Have the accountant specify which approved changes belong in the financial books.

## Create a Repeatable Bookkeeping Schedule

### Give daily and weekly work a defined scope

Use daily work to capture documents and flag exceptions. File supplier invoices, preserve the relevant collection summaries, and identify missing reports. Keep the task focused enough that it fits into the practice’s operating day.

Use a weekly review to work through the open questions. Check unmatched deposits, uncategorized transactions, invoices awaiting approval, and supplier credits awaiting confirmation.

Assign each item to someone who can obtain the missing information.

A useful exception note describes the issue and next action: “Supplier credit confirmation needed; purchasing lead to request it.” Avoid vague entries such as “check accounting,” which leave the next person to reconstruct the problem.

If you do the books yourself, maintain the same queue. Treat it as the agenda for your next bookkeeping session and your next discussion with the accountant.

### Close the month with a documented sequence

Set a recurring monthly close routine with a target review date and clear prerequisites. Use a checklist such as this:

1. Gather statements and supporting reports for the period.
2. Review imported transactions and resolve coding questions.
3. Reconcile bank and credit card accounts.
4. Review deposit matching and settlement exceptions.
5. Check supplier bills, credits, and payroll support.
6. Review receivables and inventory questions with the responsible people.
7. Obtain necessary accounting adjustments and explanations.
8. Prepare the financial reports and owner review notes.

The checklist should show who prepared each item, who reviewed it, and what remains open. If the same person performs both roles, record the review separately from the initial entry work.

Save the reviewed reports with their reporting period and accounting basis clearly identified. If an unresolved item affects how a report should be interpreted, include that explanation with the report.

When a later correction changes a previously reviewed period, ask your accountant how to document the change and whether replacement reports are needed.

## Review Reports and Choose Tools Deliberately

### Read the reports with specific questions

Ask your accountant to walk through the profit-and-loss statement and balance sheet using your practice’s categories. Use that discussion to establish which supporting schedules you should review alongside them.

Begin with questions about the records: Are income categories complete? Are unfamiliar expenses explained? Are there unresolved supplier credits? Are inventory adjustments supported? Which balances require further investigation?

Then examine changes across periods. Ask whether an apparent change reflects business activity, timing, a correction, or a coding change. Write down the explanation and any follow-up decision.

Be careful when comparing your practice with a generalized expense benchmark. Optometric Management explains that expense benchmarks can vary with the mix of medical eye care and refraction services.

[Optometric Management: Software for Financial Health](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2019/november/software-for-financial-health).

Start with a clear understanding of your own categories and service mix before deciding whether an outside comparison is relevant.

### Evaluate software through an actual bookkeeping task

When assessing accounting software or an integration, ask for a demonstration using synthetic business data. Follow a transaction through import, categorization, matching, correction, and reporting.

Check whether you can identify the source of an entry, attach appropriate business documentation, review changes, and produce the reports your accountant expects.

If you operate multiple locations, demonstrate the location reporting you intend to use.

Write down which system supplies each report and who checks the transfer between systems. For any automated rule, specify how exceptions will be reviewed and who may change the rule.

Also establish a way to retrieve your accounting records and reports. Ask about access arrangements, export options, and the practical steps involved in changing providers.

Make these part of the setup discussion rather than leaving them until a handoff is necessary.

## Make the Routine Work for Your Practice

### For a solo owner, define what you retain

If you handle the books yourself, decide which tasks you will perform and which questions go to your accountant.

A workable division might have you gather documents, review ordinary transactions, and investigate missing information while the accountant determines unfamiliar accounting treatment.

Keep a running question list. Bring the supporting documents and explain what you have already checked. Ask for written instructions when an answer will apply to future transactions.

Choose a recurring time for reviewing the books and a separate time for reading the finished reports. Give the review a concrete endpoint: explained balances, documented open items, and assigned next actions.

If a task repeatedly remains unfinished, reconsider its owner or scope. Decide whether it needs clearer instructions, different access, or outside assistance.

### For a group or delegated team, define the handoff

When several people contribute, write a responsibility map covering document collection, transaction entry, reconciliation, billing follow-up, accountant review, and owner approval. Name a backup for tasks that cannot wait for someone’s return.

For shared bookkeeping support, describe the deliverables precisely. Specify the reports to prepare, the questions to escalate, the approvals required, and where supporting business records belong.

Use a regular handoff note that identifies completed work, unresolved items, and decisions needed from the owner.

Keep location-specific questions attached to the relevant location while maintaining consistent accounting instructions across the group.
