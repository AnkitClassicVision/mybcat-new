Dental service organizations and multi-location healthcare groups face a unique challenge when measuring call center performance: the metrics that work for single-location practices often fail to capture the operational complexity of a distributed network. COOs and VPs of Operations need benchmarks that account for variability across locations, patient populations, and service lines, not generic contact center standards borrowed from retail or financial services.

This guide breaks down the KPIs that matter most for enterprise healthcare call centers, explains how to establish meaningful benchmarks for your organization, and outlines the operational frameworks that translate measurement into improvement.

## Why Healthcare Call Center Benchmarking Differs from Other Industries

Healthcare contact centers operate under constraints that most industries never encounter. Patient access affects clinical outcomes, regulatory requirements shape every interaction, and the consequences of poor performance extend far beyond customer satisfaction scores.

### The Patient Access Imperative

When patients call a healthcare organization, they are often navigating uncertainty about symptoms, insurance coverage, or treatment options. The speed and quality of that initial interaction shapes their perception of the entire care experience.

Research published in peer-reviewed journals demonstrates that a call center's average speed of answer is inversely associated with patients' perceptions of their ability to access urgent care appointments in a timely manner. This finding underscores why healthcare organizations cannot simply adopt benchmarks from industries where the stakes of a delayed response are purely transactional.

For DSOs specifically, the call center often represents the first touchpoint in the patient journey. A prospective patient calling to schedule a cleaning or consultation forms an impression of the entire organization based on how that call is handled. The operational implications extend to case acceptance, treatment plan adherence, and long-term patient retention.

### Regulatory and Compliance Considerations

Healthcare call centers must navigate requirements that do not apply to most contact center operations. Patient information handling, appointment scheduling protocols, and clinical triage guidelines all introduce complexity that affects staffing models, training requirements, and performance expectations.

Multi-location healthcare groups must also ensure consistency across their network. A patient calling one location should receive the same quality of service as a patient calling another, a challenge that requires standardized protocols and centralized quality assurance processes.

### The Multi-Location Complexity Factor

Single-location practices can optimize their call handling based on one patient population, one provider schedule, and one set of operational constraints. DSOs and healthcare groups with 3+ locations must account for:

- Variation in call volume patterns across markets
- Different payer mixes and insurance verification requirements
- Multiple practice management systems and scheduling workflows
- Location-specific service offerings and provider availability
- Regional differences in patient expectations and communication preferences

These factors make direct comparison between locations challenging and require benchmarking approaches that normalize for legitimate operational differences.

## Core KPIs for DSO and Healthcare Group Call Centers

Effective call center measurement for enterprise healthcare requires a balanced scorecard approach. No single metric captures the full picture of operational performance, patient experience, and financial impact.

### Access and Responsiveness Metrics

The foundational metrics for any healthcare call center relate to whether patients can reach the organization and how quickly their needs are addressed.

**Average Speed of Answer (ASA)** measures the time between when a call enters the queue and when an agent answers. For healthcare organizations, this metric carries particular weight because delays in reaching a practice can affect patient perceptions of care accessibility.

**Abandonment Rate** captures the percentage of callers who hang up before reaching an agent. High abandonment rates often indicate understaffing, inefficient call routing, or excessive hold times. For healthcare organizations, abandoned calls may represent missed appointments, delayed care, or patients who seek services elsewhere.

**Service Level** typically expresses the percentage of calls answered within a target time threshold. Healthcare organizations commonly set service level targets that reflect the urgency expectations of their patient population.

### Efficiency and Productivity Metrics

Operational efficiency metrics help organizations understand how effectively their call center resources are deployed.

**Average Handle Time (AHT)** encompasses the total duration of a call, including talk time and any post-call work. Healthcare contact centers must balance efficiency against thoroughness, rushing through insurance verification or appointment scheduling to improve AHT can create downstream problems.

Industry analysis of critical healthcare contact center metrics identifies average handle time as a key performance indicator, but emphasizes that healthcare organizations should interpret this metric in the context of call complexity and patient needs rather than pursuing arbitrary reduction targets.

**First Call Resolution (FCR)** measures the percentage of calls where the patient's need is fully addressed without requiring a callback, transfer, or follow-up. High FCR rates indicate effective agent training, appropriate call routing, and sufficient agent authority to resolve issues.

**Occupancy Rate** reflects the percentage of logged-in time that agents spend handling calls or performing related work. While high occupancy indicates efficient resource utilization, excessively high rates can lead to agent burnout and quality degradation.

### Patient Experience Metrics

Patient experience metrics capture the qualitative dimensions of call center performance that access and efficiency metrics may miss.

**Patient Satisfaction (PSAT)** typically measured through post-call surveys, reflects the patient's perception of the interaction. For healthcare organizations, satisfaction scores should be interpreted alongside clinical outcomes and access metrics.

**Net Promoter Score (NPS)** measures the likelihood that a patient would recommend the organization based on their call center experience. This metric can serve as a leading indicator of patient retention and referral patterns.

**Quality Assurance Scores** from call monitoring and evaluation programs provide insight into whether agents are following protocols, demonstrating appropriate empathy, and accurately handling clinical and administrative information.

### Financial Impact Metrics

For enterprise healthcare organizations, call center performance directly affects revenue and operational costs.

**Conversion Rate** for new patient calls measures the percentage of inquiry calls that result in scheduled appointments. This metric is particularly relevant for DSOs, where patient acquisition represents a significant portion of marketing investment.

**Revenue Per Call** helps organizations understand the financial value generated through call center interactions, including new patient bookings, treatment plan discussions, and appointment confirmations.

**Cost Per Call** provides a baseline for understanding operational efficiency and enables comparison against alternative service delivery models.

## Establishing Meaningful Benchmarks for Your Organization

Generic industry benchmarks provide a starting point for performance evaluation, but enterprise healthcare organizations need benchmarking approaches tailored to their specific operational context.

### Internal Benchmarking Across Locations

For DSOs and healthcare groups with multiple locations, internal benchmarking offers the most actionable insights. Comparing performance across your own network controls for organizational factors while revealing location-specific opportunities.

Effective internal benchmarking requires:

**Normalization for legitimate differences.** A location with high Medicare penetration will have different call characteristics than one serving primarily commercial insurance patients. Benchmarking should account for these structural differences rather than penalizing locations for factors outside their control.

**Consistent measurement methodology.** All locations must define and capture metrics the same way. If one location counts transferred calls differently than another, comparisons become meaningless.

**Peer groupings based on operational similarity.** Grouping locations by size, patient volume, or service mix enables more meaningful comparison than organization-wide averages.

MGMA analysis on healthcare benchmarking emphasizes the importance of using comparative data to drive operational decisions, covering patient access metrics and performance standards that apply across practice types.

### External Benchmarking Considerations

External benchmarks from industry associations and research organizations provide context for internal performance data. However, healthcare organizations should approach external benchmarks with appropriate caution.

Industry-standard benchmarking resources offer KPIs including hours of operation, wait times, appointment availability, and call volume metrics for healthcare practices. These benchmarks are most useful when the comparison population matches your organization's characteristics.

For DSOs specifically, benchmarking against dental-specific sources provides more relevant context than general healthcare contact center data. Industry analysis of key performance indicators for dental service organizations covers patient acquisition, retention, and operational efficiency metrics that reflect the unique characteristics of dental practice operations.

### Setting Targets That Drive Improvement

Benchmark data informs target setting, but effective targets require additional considerations:

**Stretch without unrealism.** Targets should push performance improvement without being so aggressive that they demoralize staff or encourage counterproductive behaviors.

**Balance across metrics.** Pursuing aggressive ASA targets at the expense of quality, or FCR targets at the expense of efficiency, creates organizational dysfunction. Effective target frameworks maintain balance across the scorecard.

**Connection to business outcomes.** The most meaningful targets connect call center performance to patient experience, clinical outcomes, and financial results. Improving a metric that does not affect these outcomes provides limited value.

## Operational Frameworks for Performance Improvement

Measurement without action provides limited value. Enterprise healthcare organizations need operational frameworks that translate benchmark data into performance improvement.

### Staffing and Scheduling Optimization

Call center performance begins with having the right number of agents with the right skills available at the right times.

MGMA guidance on healthcare call center best practices includes staffing models that account for call volume patterns, agent skill requirements, and service level targets. For multi-location organizations, centralized staffing optimization can improve resource utilization while maintaining service quality.

**Workforce management systems** enable data-driven scheduling that matches staffing levels to predicted call volumes. These systems become more valuable as organizations scale, since manual scheduling becomes increasingly difficult to optimize across multiple locations and time zones.

**Cross-training programs** expand the flexibility of your agent pool by enabling agents to handle multiple call types or serve multiple locations. This flexibility becomes particularly valuable during volume spikes or unexpected absences.

**Overflow and backup strategies** ensure that service levels are maintained even when call volumes exceed predictions. These strategies might include cross-trained agents from other departments, scheduled overtime, or partnerships with external answering services.

### Technology Integration and Automation

Modern call center technology can improve both efficiency and patient experience when implemented thoughtfully.

**Intelligent call routing** directs calls to the most appropriate agent or location based on caller information, call reason, and agent skills. For multi-location healthcare groups, sophisticated routing can balance workload across locations while ensuring patients reach agents equipped to help them.

**Integration with practice management systems** enables agents to access scheduling, patient records, and insurance information without toggling between applications. This integration reduces handle time while improving accuracy and patient experience.

**Automated self-service options** can handle routine requests like appointment confirmations or directions, freeing agents to focus on calls requiring human judgment. However, healthcare organizations must design self-service carefully to ensure patients with urgent needs can quickly reach a live agent.

### Quality Assurance and Continuous Improvement

Sustained performance improvement requires systematic quality assurance and feedback processes.

**Call monitoring programs** provide insight into actual agent performance that metrics alone cannot capture. Regular monitoring identifies training opportunities, protocol gaps, and systemic issues affecting performance.

**Calibration sessions** ensure that quality evaluators apply standards consistently. For multi-location organizations, cross-location calibration prevents drift in quality expectations.

**Agent coaching and development** translates quality insights into individual performance improvement. Effective coaching programs balance accountability with support, helping agents develop skills rather than simply documenting deficiencies.

**Root cause analysis** for performance issues prevents organizations from addressing symptoms while underlying problems persist. When a metric falls below target, effective organizations investigate why before implementing solutions.

## Building a Measurement Culture Across Your Network

For DSOs and healthcare groups with 3+ locations, consistent measurement requires more than standardized reports. It requires a culture where data drives decisions at every level of the organization.

### Executive Visibility and Accountability

Call center performance should have visibility at the executive level, with clear accountability for results.

**Regular performance reviews** ensure that call center metrics receive appropriate attention alongside clinical and financial measures. These reviews should include both current performance and trend analysis.

**Clear ownership** for performance outcomes prevents diffusion of responsibility. Whether accountability sits with a VP of Operations, a centralized patient access leader, or regional directors, someone must be responsible for results.

**Resource allocation decisions** should reflect the strategic importance of call center performance. Organizations that treat the call center as a cost center to minimize rather than a strategic asset to optimize often underinvest in the capabilities needed for excellence.

### Location-Level Engagement

While centralized measurement and standards are essential, location-level engagement drives day-to-day performance.

**Local dashboards** that display relevant metrics help location teams understand their performance and track improvement efforts. These dashboards should emphasize metrics that location teams can directly influence.

**Recognition programs** that celebrate improvement and excellence reinforce the importance of call center performance. Recognition is most effective when it acknowledges both absolute performance and improvement trajectory.

**Feedback channels** enable location teams to surface operational issues, share best practices, and propose improvements. Centralized measurement should inform these conversations rather than replace them.

### Continuous Evolution of Measurement

Effective measurement frameworks evolve as organizations mature and operational contexts change.

**Regular metric review** ensures that the organization continues measuring what matters. Metrics that made sense during rapid growth may become less relevant as the organization matures.

**New metric development** addresses emerging operational needs. As organizations adopt new technologies or service models, measurement frameworks should evolve accordingly.

**Benchmark refreshes** ensure that targets reflect current industry context. External benchmarks that were appropriate several years ago may no longer represent relevant performance standards.

## Connecting Call Center Performance to Enterprise Outcomes

For COOs and VPs of Operations, call center metrics matter because they connect to broader organizational outcomes. Making these connections explicit helps justify investment and focus improvement efforts.

### Patient Acquisition and Retention

Call center performance directly affects the patient funnel. Prospective patients who cannot reach the organization or receive poor service often seek care elsewhere. Existing patients who struggle to schedule appointments or resolve issues may not return.

Tracking conversion rates for new patient calls and retention rates for existing patients helps quantify the revenue impact of call center performance. These connections make the business case for call center investment concrete.

### Operational Efficiency Across the Network

Call center operations interact with clinical and administrative functions throughout the organization. Poor call handling creates downstream work for clinical staff, billing departments, and practice managers.

Measuring these interactions, such as the rate of scheduling errors that require correction or insurance verification issues that delay treatment, reveals the full operational impact of call center performance.

### Brand and Reputation

For healthcare organizations competing in local markets, call center interactions shape brand perception. Patients who have positive experiences become advocates; those who have negative experiences share their frustrations.

Connecting patient satisfaction and NPS data to referral patterns and online reviews helps organizations understand how call center performance affects reputation and market position.
