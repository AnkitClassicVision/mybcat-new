Selling, recapitalizing, or selectively acquiring an optometry group is not a routine listing exercise. A broker will shape the buyer universe, the way operating results are explained, the pressure applied during negotiations, and the quality of information moving through the process. For a group with three or more locations, that work affects far more than the headline valuation. It can affect leadership continuity, staff confidence, buyer diligence burden, and the operating flexibility left after closing.

The right broker is not the person with the longest list of contacts or the lowest quoted percentage. It is the advisor whose process matches your transaction, whose buyer relationships are credible, and whose team can present the group as a transferable operating platform. This guide gives COOs, operating partners, and finance leaders a practical way to make that selection.

## Why does broker selection matter for a multi-location optometry group?

At group scale, buyers are not evaluating a collection of exam lanes and optical inventory. They are evaluating whether the organization can keep operating when ownership changes. That means the story must connect location-level economics to shared services, management cadence, clinician coverage, patient access, technology, and the policies that make performance repeatable.

A general business broker may understand a sale process but miss the operating details that distinguish an optometry platform from a set of unrelated businesses. A healthcare-focused advisor should understand the practical implications of provider relationships, payer arrangements, state ownership rules, data handling, and the transition work required after a transaction. The American Optometric Association's guidance on [selling a practice to a private equity firm](https://www.aoa.org/news/practice-management/perfect-your-practice/selling-your-practice-to-a-private-equity-firm) is a useful reminder that ownership changes require careful professional and business planning, not just a price discussion.

Broker selection also changes the workload imposed on your team. A well-run process establishes a clear diligence calendar, controlled access to information, buyer qualification standards, and a single version of key operating facts. A weak process leaves site leaders answering inconsistent questions, creates avoidable rework, and can expose internal disagreement before the management team is ready to address it.

For a view of the operating systems buyers will examine, start with MyBCAT's [enterprise healthcare operations resources](/enterprise/). That context helps an executive team judge whether a broker understands the difference between a strong patient-access model and a slide claiming that centralization exists.

## What kind of broker does your transaction require?

Begin with the transaction, not the broker's brand. A group pursuing a full exit, a minority recapitalization, a regional acquisition program, or a divestiture of selected locations needs different expertise, buyer sets, and engagement terms. Put the intended outcome in one sentence before taking introductory calls.

For a full platform sale, the broker should have experience running a confidential process with financial and strategic buyers who can evaluate a multi-site healthcare organization. The team needs to understand management rollover, post-close roles, transition services, and the questions a buyer's operating partner will ask. A broker who has only sold standalone practices may be capable, but should be able to show how their process changes for a platform transaction.

For a partial divestiture, the central issue is operational separation. You need an advisor who can help define which shared services, systems, vendor contracts, and leaders remain with the retained group. The deal story must explain how both organizations function on day one. This is where an early review of your [healthcare M&A integration plan](/blog/healthcare-operations-ma-integration/) can reveal work that belongs in the transaction scope rather than after it.

For an acquisition program, evaluate the broker as a sourcing and screening partner. Ask how opportunities are qualified before your executives spend time, how the broker handles confidential outreach, and who owns the handoff from a signed letter of intent to the integration team. The right advisor is useful only if their deal flow matches the markets, practice characteristics, and integration capacity your group has defined.

## How should you test a broker's optometry and healthcare experience?

Ask for evidence tied to transactions that resemble yours. A polished credentials deck is not enough. Request anonymized examples from the past several years that show location count, transaction type, buyer category, the broker's role, and the material issue that had to be resolved. The purpose is not to collect confidential transaction details. It is to learn whether the broker can explain a real operating problem and the work required to address it.

In interviews, test for fluency in the questions that matter to optometry groups: clinician and key-staff retention, practice-management and EHR variation, optical operations, payer and contract considerations, location-level reporting, and the handoff between a central access team and individual sites. A credible broker does not need to provide legal advice. They should know when a question belongs with healthcare counsel, tax advisors, or a compliance leader and should have a disciplined way to coordinate those specialists.

Ask each finalist to describe a transaction where the initial presentation did not match the diligence record. Listen for a practical answer: how the gap was identified, who owned the correction, how the buyer was informed, and what was changed in the process. That answer is more revealing than a generic claim of healthcare expertise.

Also verify how confidential information is handled. Deal materials can include sensitive financial, workforce, and operational information. Require a defined data-room workflow, permission controls, and a named owner for buyer access. A process that relies on forwarded attachments and informal tracking is not appropriate for a multi-location group.

## How do you evaluate buyer reach without accepting vague claims?

“Deep buyer relationships” is not a selection criterion until it becomes observable. Ask a broker to describe the buyer categories they would approach, why each category fits the asset, and how they decide who does not receive access. They do not need to disclose other clients or confidential negotiations. They should be able to explain their qualification logic and the sequence of outreach.

Buyer reach has three components. First, the broker needs relevant coverage: financial sponsors, strategic buyers, and other qualified parties appropriate to your structure and geography. Second, the broker needs credibility with those buyers, so outreach earns attention rather than becoming another broad listing. Third, the broker needs process discipline, including confidentiality agreements, a controlled information release, management presentation preparation, and a way to keep interest moving without creating unnecessary operational disruption.

Ask for references from executives whose deals had similar complexity. During the reference call, ask whether the broker brought qualified parties, how accurately the team represented the work required of management, and how the broker handled a difficult diligence or negotiation moment. Do not ask a reference to reveal terms they cannot share. Focus on execution quality and whether the broker's pre-engagement promises matched the process delivered.

This exercise should also surface buyer fit. A wide list is not always better. A smaller, well-qualified field may protect confidentiality and reduce management distraction. The decision depends on your objectives, not a fixed number of interested parties.

## What should a broker prepare before your group goes to market?

The broker should lead a preparation phase that turns internal knowledge into a coherent, supportable buyer narrative. That work commonly includes a financial presentation, an operating overview, a buyer list and outreach plan, a diligence index, management presentation materials, and a process calendar. The materials should show both portfolio-level results and the drivers behind differences among locations.

Preparation should not manufacture a story. It should reconcile it. If one location uses different scheduling rules, if reporting definitions differ, or if a key workflow depends on a single leader, the broker needs to help the team identify that fact early and decide how it will be explained. Buyers can accept variation when it is understood and managed. They lose confidence when the variation appears unexpectedly during diligence.

Patient-access operations deserve particular attention because they connect demand, scheduling, follow-up, staffing, and location experience. A documented model for call handling, scheduling ownership, recall, reporting, and exceptions gives a buyer a clearer view of operating transferability. The [pre-sale operational cleanup guide](/blog/pre-sale-operational-cleanup-intake-valuation/) can help your team identify which intake artifacts are ready to share and which need work before outreach begins.

Management should review every material claim before it reaches buyers. That includes revenue presentation, adjusted earnings, provider capacity, location performance, staffing model, technology environment, and growth initiatives. Build a claim log with the source, owner, and supporting document for each important statement. This reduces the risk of a broker circulating an attractive but unsupported narrative.

## How should you compare fees and engagement terms?

Compare economics as a complete engagement, not as a single commission percentage. A fee proposal can include a retainer, a success fee, minimum fees, expenses, a tail period, an exclusivity commitment, and separate charges for valuation support, marketing materials, or data-room administration. Put each proposal into the same comparison sheet so finance and legal can see the full picture.

The percentage alone cannot tell you whether the terms align with your interests. A lower success fee may come with a broad tail, reimbursement obligations, or a process that puts too little senior attention on the engagement. A higher fee may include deeper preparation and direct senior involvement, but those promises should be written into the scope rather than accepted as sales language.

Ask how the fee changes if the transaction structure changes. For example, clarify how the broker treats rollover equity, earnouts, contingent consideration, debt assumed by a buyer, real estate, or a partial sale. Clarify when a retainer is earned, which expenses require approval, and what happens if the broker introduces a buyer but the engagement ends before a deal closes. Your counsel should review the final agreement; the broker interview is where you learn whether the commercial terms are understandable before that review begins.

The [MGMA overview of successful mergers and acquisitions](https://www.mgma.com/articles/steps-to-a-successful-merger-or-acquisition) supports the broader discipline here: transaction work benefits from early planning and clear ownership. Fee terms are part of that planning, not an administrative detail to settle at the end.

## Which due diligence capabilities should you require?

Your broker does not replace finance, legal, clinical, compliance, or operations leadership. Their job is to organize the process so those experts can respond accurately and on time. Require the broker to explain how they create a diligence request list, prioritize questions, track open items, control document versions, prepare management for buyer meetings, and maintain a decision log.

For a multi-location group, diligence needs a portfolio view and a location view. Buyers will want to understand the reasons behind different operating results, not merely see a blended total. The broker should be able to organize the material around recurring categories: financial performance, provider and staff structure, site operations, systems, key contracts, patient-access workflows, compliance controls, and integration dependencies.

Ask how the broker handles questions that should not be answered by a broker. Good advisors maintain an issue register, route each question to the right owner, and prevent informal answers from becoming deal facts. They should know when a response requires legal review, when it needs an operations validation, and when a buyer needs to be told that the answer is pending.

Do not promise results that are not documented. If your group is improving recall, scheduling, or call handling, describe the system, baseline, owner, and measurement method. ## How should post-close operations affect broker selection?

The transaction closes on a date; operational integration begins immediately. Broker selection should therefore include the broker's ability to support a clean handoff from the deal team to the operators who will carry the transition. The broker need not run integration, but should understand the work well enough to avoid commitments that create an unworkable day-one plan.

Ask finalists how they prepare management for buyer discussions about transition services, leadership roles, staff communications, technology cutovers, and patient-facing continuity. Ask what information they expect to exist before final bids and what must be resolved before signing. PwC's [M&A integration playbooks](https://www.pwc.com/us/en/services/consulting/deals/library/ma-integration-playbooks.html) emphasize that integration planning belongs alongside deal preparation. That is especially relevant when multiple locations share staff, systems, and patient-access workflows.

Earnout and rollover structures require the same care. A broker should help management identify operating metrics, allocation rules, decision rights, and reporting definitions that deserve attention before a post-close performance commitment is accepted. The broker cannot determine whether a term is legally or financially appropriate. They can make sure the right advisors and operating owners see it before the transaction calendar makes a thoughtful review difficult.

For teams building the first 90 days after close, use the [healthcare acquisition intake sprint](/blog/90-day-integration-playbook-healthcare-acquisitions/) to translate the deal plan into specific ownership, workflow, and reporting decisions.

## What belongs in the broker RFP and finalist interview?

Use a short RFP to make the comparison fair. It should describe the transaction objective, location count, broad operating profile, decision timeline, required services, and the information you expect the broker to provide. Avoid sharing detailed confidential data until the group has completed an initial qualification step and the appropriate confidentiality protections are in place.

Require finalists to answer these questions in writing:

- Which comparable multi-location healthcare or optometry transactions have you led, and what was your role?
- Which buyer categories fit our transaction, and how will you qualify them before disclosure?
- What will you produce during preparation, who will do the work, and what must our management team supply?
- How will you run the data room, management meetings, diligence log, and buyer communications?
- What healthcare counsel, tax, valuation, and operational specialists do you coordinate with, and when do you bring them in?
- What are all fees, expenses, exclusivity terms, tail provisions, and treatment of contingent consideration?
- How will you support handoff to the post-close operating team without assuming responsibilities that belong to management or counsel?

The interview should test the written response with a scenario from your own operating reality. For example, ask how the broker would position a group whose locations have different scheduling systems but a shared central access function. The strongest answer will separate facts from assumptions, name the information needed, and describe a path to a buyer-ready explanation.

## How do you make the final selection decision?

Use a weighted scorecard, but do not let a score hide a material concern. Score each broker on relevant transaction experience, buyer fit, preparation process, senior-team involvement, diligence discipline, fee clarity, healthcare coordination, and transition awareness. Give each category a written definition before interviews so different evaluators are judging the same thing.

Then conduct a final risk review. Ask whether the broker has any conflict, concentration risk, capability gap, or engagement term that would matter if the deal becomes more complex than expected. If a finalist is weak in one area but otherwise compelling, document the mitigation: a defined workstream, a named specialist, a revised engagement term, or a clear internal owner.

The recommendation should be easy to explain to the board, investment committee, or executive team: this broker fits this transaction because their evidence, process, and commercial terms address the group’s highest risks. That is a stronger basis for selection than a familiar name or a fee comparison alone.
