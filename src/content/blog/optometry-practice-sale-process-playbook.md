Selling an optometry practice is both a business decision and a personal transition. Alongside the terms of a sale, you may be thinking about your patients, your staff, the care you have built, and what your own work will look like afterward.

An effective sale process gives each of those questions room. It begins before you speak with a prospective buyer and continues after the transaction closes.

This optometry practice sale process playbook walks a single-office owner through the decisions, preparation, diligence, and handoff that can make the process easier to manage.

The American Optometric Association (AOA) discusses preparing a practice, maintaining revenue, finding a buyer, and planning the owner’s post-sale role in its guidance on [practice transitions](https://www.aoa.org/news/practice-management/perfect-your-practice/transition-right).

Use those themes as a starting point, then work with advisers who can address your circumstances.

## Define what a successful sale means to you

### Set your personal and professional priorities

Before discussing a sale price or approaching buyers, write down why you are considering a transition.

You may want to retire, reduce administrative responsibilities, continue practicing without ownership duties, or create a path to ownership for an associate. Your answer will shape which opportunities deserve attention.

Separate your priorities into what you need, what you would prefer, and where you have flexibility.

For example, continuing to see patients may be essential to you, while a particular job title may matter less. A buyer cannot design a workable proposal around preferences that have never been discussed.

Also consider how much uncertainty you can accept. A transition that depends on a continuing role, future decisions by a buyer, or an extended handoff may feel very different from a clean departure. Ask your advisers to help you understand how each proposed arrangement would work in practice.

### Decide what you want to protect

Think about the patient experience, the staff culture, and the clinical approach you hope to carry forward. These goals do not automatically translate into contractual commitments, but identifying them early helps you ask better questions.

For staff, you might care about clear communication, continuity of responsibilities, or how changes will be introduced. For patients, you may want a thoughtful introduction to the new owner and a clear way to ask questions.

Write down the specific outcomes you want to discuss with buyers instead of relying on broad assurances that nothing will change.

The AOA’s discussion of [private equity sales](https://www.aoa.org/news/practice-management/perfect-your-practice/selling-your-practice-to-a-private-equity-firm) highlights questions about management control, patient care, and advice from legal and accounting professionals.

Those topics can also help frame conversations with other types of buyers.

## Assemble your sale team and preparation plan

### Choose advisers who understand your objectives

A practice sale can involve business, financial, legal, tax, and operational questions. Consider who will help you evaluate the transaction and coordinate the work.

Depending on your needs, that may include an attorney, an accountant, an appraiser, and a practice broker or other transaction adviser.

Ask each prospective adviser about their experience with optometry practice transitions, the scope of their work, and how they communicate.

Discuss fees and possible conflicts of interest before engaging them. It is also useful to identify who will lead the process and who will review each major decision.

Your advisers can help you compare the implications of different proposals, but they need to know what matters to you. Share your written priorities with them and revisit those priorities when a promising offer creates pressure to move quickly.

### Build a preparation list before approaching buyers

Create a working list of the documents, decisions, and operational questions you expect to address. Assign an owner to each item, note where the information is kept, and identify anything that needs professional review.

The list is a management tool, not a promise that every issue must be resolved before a conversation begins.

Start with financial statements, tax returns, the lease, equipment information, significant agreements, and a description of how the practice operates.

*Optometric Management* discusses financial statements, tax returns, profitability, confidentiality, and sale goals in its [practice sale guidance](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2016/november/ready-to-buy-or-sell-a-practice).

As you organize materials, distinguish between records you can use for preparation and information that should be shared only after an appropriate review. Your attorney can help establish a process for confidential discussions and document access.

## Prepare the practice for buyer review

### Organize financial and business records

A buyer needs to understand the business they are considering. Gather the financial records your advisers recommend and check that they are understandable and consistent.

If an unusual expense, owner-related item, or change in the business needs explanation, document the context rather than expecting a buyer to infer it.

Ask your accountant to help you review questions about profitability and any proposed adjustments. An explanation should be supported by the underlying records and should make clear which items reflect the current business and which depend on assumptions about the future.

Keep a list of open questions alongside the documents. If a record is incomplete or a discrepancy remains unresolved, tell your advisers.

A clear account of what is known and what still needs review gives a prospective buyer a more useful starting point than a polished but incomplete presentation.

### Review the lease, equipment, and agreements

The practice location and the tools used to provide care are part of the buyer’s evaluation. Gather the lease and any related documents, then ask your attorney to review provisions relevant to a potential transaction.

Do not assume that an arrangement will continue on the same terms simply because it is working today.

Prepare an equipment inventory that describes what the practice uses and identifies related agreements or service arrangements. Review significant vendor and business agreements with your advisers as well. Note questions about ownership, obligations, renewal, and any proposed changes.

The AOA’s [buyer guidance](https://www.aoa.org/practice/owning-a-practice/buying-a-practice) identifies the lease, equipment, goodwill, financial records, and transition arrangements as areas a buyer may evaluate.

Looking at these materials from the buyer’s perspective can help you prepare answers without losing sight of your own interests.

## Make daily operations understandable

### Document the work that keeps the office moving

A single-office practice often relies on knowledge held by its owner and experienced team members. Write down how routine work is handled, including appointment requests, phone coverage, intake, billing follow-up, supply ordering, and communication with patients.

Focus on what someone taking responsibility for the work needs to know: who owns each task, where instructions are maintained, what happens when an issue cannot be resolved, and which vendors or systems support the process.

Keep the documentation practical. A concise workflow that staff can use is more valuable than a lengthy manual that no one maintains.

This review can also reveal decisions that consistently return to the owner. Consider whether responsibilities can be clarified or documented before a sale. Any change should fit your practice and be communicated to the people doing the work.

### Continue serving patients during the process

Sale preparation can compete with the demands of running an office. Set aside time for transaction work while keeping routine responsibilities visible. Decide who handles buyer requests, who maintains the preparation list, and when advisers should be brought into a question.

Keep staff and patient communications appropriate to the stage of the process. Premature or inconsistent messages can create confusion, while waiting until the last moment can leave people without answers they need. Plan the timing and content with your advisers and, when appropriate, the buyer.

The aim is to make the practice’s ordinary work understandable and sustainable through a period of change. It is not necessary to redesign every workflow before considering a sale.

## Explore buyer options and compare offers

### Consider the buyer and the transition model

Potential paths may include a sale to an associate, another optometrist, or an organization seeking to acquire a practice. Each prospective buyer may have a different vision for ownership, management, staffing, and the seller’s future role.

Ask how the buyer expects the practice to operate after closing.

Who will make decisions about staffing and daily operations? What role is envisioned for you? How will the buyer introduce themselves to patients and employees? What assumptions are they making about the lease, systems, and existing agreements?

A gradual transition is another possibility to discuss when it fits both parties.

An [*Optometric Management* account of a transition to an associate](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2019/december/transition-out-of-practice) describes identifying a successor and planning a gradual ownership change.

It is an example of one path, not a template that will suit every practice.

### Look beyond the headline offer

Ask your advisers to help you compare the full terms of each proposal. Consider the proposed structure, how and when payment would be made, conditions that must be satisfied, responsibilities each party would retain, and the work expected of you after closing.

Review the assumptions behind any future obligations. If an offer includes a continuing employment or consulting role, ask for clarity on duties, decision-making authority, compensation terms, and how either party can address a disagreement.

If your goals include staff or patient continuity, discuss how those intentions will be reflected in the transaction and transition documents.

An offer that initially looks attractive may depend on terms that need further negotiation.

*Optometric Management* discusses potential pitfalls involving valuation, liabilities, arbitration clauses, and changes to staff and operations in its article on [practice sale transactions](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2024/novemberdecember/preventing-practice-sales-transaction-pitfalls).

## Manage due diligence and protect sensitive information

### Set a clear process for buyer requests

During due diligence, a prospective buyer reviews information about the practice and may ask follow-up questions. Establish a central request list so you and your advisers can see what has been requested, who is responsible for responding, and whether a question remains open.

Use a controlled process for providing documents. Confirm who is authorized to receive information and consult your advisers about confidentiality and the appropriate scope of disclosure. Keep explanations consistent with the records, and correct an error promptly if you discover one.

Not every request has to be answered immediately or in the form first proposed. If a request is unclear, ask the buyer what decision the information will support. Your advisers can help determine an appropriate response and whether a question requires additional review.

### Plan patient-record questions with qualified advisers

A practice sale raises sensitive questions about patient information, including what may be discussed during diligence and how records will be handled in a transition.

Do not send patient information to a prospective buyer simply because it appears on a request list. Ask your attorney and other qualified advisers to review the circumstances, the proposed disclosures, and the planned handoff.

The AOA’s [HIPAA frequently asked questions](https://www.aoa.org/AOA/Documents/Practice%20Management/Coding/HIPAA.FAQ.pdf) address sale discussions, due diligence, and disclosures to third parties.

Its [case study on selling a practice](https://www.aoa.org/AOA/Documents/Education/Education_PDFs/Case%20Study%2016-Selling%20a%20Practice.pdf) also addresses patient notification and transfer of records.

Use those resources to prepare questions, not as a substitute for advice tailored to the transaction. Your plan should identify who will make decisions about records, who will communicate with patients, and how questions will be handled.

## Negotiate the agreement and prepare to close

### Resolve the issues that matter before signing

As negotiations progress, return to your original priorities and compare them with the proposed documents. Ask your attorney and accountant to explain terms you do not understand and how the documents address the responsibilities, risks, and expectations you discussed.

Pay particular attention to the assets and obligations included in the transaction, any conditions to closing, and what happens if a condition is not met. If your future role is part of the arrangement, review the related terms alongside the sale agreement so you understand how they fit together.

Keep a written list of unresolved issues and decisions. Some questions may be routine; others may change whether the deal still meets your needs. Give your advisers time to investigate material concerns instead of relying on an informal promise that details can be settled later.

### Coordinate the closing work

A closing plan should identify the tasks each party expects to complete, the documents or approvals involved, and who will confirm completion. Your advisers can help coordinate transaction requirements, while you and the buyer can prepare for operational changes that depend on the sale proceeding.

Review the practical handoffs as well: access to systems, vendor contacts, equipment information, office procedures, and communication responsibilities.

Discuss how outstanding work will be identified and assigned. Avoid assuming that a signed agreement alone tells each person what to do when ownership changes.

If a significant issue emerges late in the process, pause long enough to understand it with your advisers. The appropriate response depends on the issue and the transaction; a deadline does not make an unanswered question disappear.

## Support staff and patients through the handoff

### Give the team a clear communication plan

Work with the buyer on what employees will be told, who will deliver the message, and how questions will be answered. Explain what is known, acknowledge decisions that are still pending, and avoid making promises that have not been agreed upon.

Staff may want to know about reporting relationships, daily responsibilities, and whom to approach with concerns. Prepare managers or designated contacts to respond consistently. If an answer depends on a later decision, say when and how the team can expect an update.

A useful handoff also makes space for the knowledge employees hold. Invite the people responsible for daily work to identify practical details the new owner should understand, such as how requests are routed or how unusual situations are escalated.

### Introduce the new owner and define your role

Agree on a patient communication plan with the buyer and your advisers. Consider what patients need to know about the change, where they can direct questions, and how the practice will explain any changes to ordinary office procedures.

Your advisers can help review questions about notices and records in the context of the sale.

If you will remain involved, put your responsibilities in writing. Define how you will introduce the buyer, transfer operational knowledge, handle patient-facing work, and communicate about unresolved issues.

If you plan to leave, identify the information and contacts the new owner needs before your departure.

A transition is complete when the agreed responsibilities have been handed over and the people involved know how to move forward. Review the handoff list with the buyer and address remaining questions directly.
