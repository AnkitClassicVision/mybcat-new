For an eye care group with 3+ locations, a postcard campaign needs decisions about eligibility, communication preferences, appointment availability, and follow-up before the mailing leaves the practice.

This eye care recall postcard playbook gives COOs, VPs of Operations, and buying committees a practical framework for those decisions. Use it to establish a shared process while keeping clinical recall instructions and location-specific scheduling requirements with the appropriate teams.

The operating goal is straightforward: every released mailing should have an approved audience, a clear scheduling route, an accountable owner, and a defined next action. Evaluate postcards within that complete workflow, with reporting that distinguishes outreach activity from appointments booked and visits completed.

## Establish ownership and recall scope

### Start with the documented recall instruction

Build the campaign around the practice’s documented return recommendation. Ask clinical leadership to approve which recall categories belong in routine outreach and which require a separate clinical follow-up process. Let the clinical team determine the appropriate return interval.

Optometric Management’s [“Implement a Patient Recall System”](https://optometricmanagement.com/issues/2021/march/implement-a-patient-recall-system) describes establishing EHR recall triggers, assigning staff follow-up, and reviewing recall reports monthly.

It also describes mailing postcards the month before an expected exam. Treat that mailing schedule as an operational example; select campaign timing around the documented recall instruction and the group’s scheduling process.

Write down what makes a record eligible for each campaign. Include the relevant recall status, location assignment, appointment status, and communication preference. Keep routine recall, missed-appointment recovery, and dormant-patient outreach distinguishable so staff can apply the correct instructions.

### Assign decision rights across the group

Designate a group owner who maintains the playbook and a location owner who resolves local exceptions. Give the scheduling team responsibility for responding to patients and recording the next action.

Use a responsibility table during implementation:

| Responsibility | Recommended accountable role | Required handoff |
|---|---|---|
| Recall criteria | Clinical leadership | Approved campaign eligibility rules |
| Campaign coordination | Group operations owner | Release schedule and unresolved issues |
| Location readiness | Location manager | Appointment availability and routing instructions |
| Communication preferences | Designated privacy or records lead | Approved handling of mailing exceptions |
| Patient response | Scheduling team | Booking status or documented next action |
| Campaign reporting | Operations reporting owner | Consistent definitions and reconciled results |

Name a backup for each operational responsibility. Specify who can pause a mailing when eligibility, routing, or communication preferences remain unresolved. A shared inbox can receive questions, but assign a person to resolve them.

## Prepare the mailing list before release

### Reconcile eligibility with current appointment status

Use the approved recall system as the starting point. Before releasing a mailing, reconcile the proposed audience with current scheduling information. Establish how the team will handle records that have already booked, no longer meet campaign criteria, or appear in another active outreach queue.

For patients associated with multiple locations, define which location owns the recall and which locations may offer appointments. Record that decision in the approved workflow so local teams do not independently create competing outreach.

Give uncertain records an exception status. For example, a missing recall instruction should go to the designated reviewer rather than being assigned a return interval by campaign staff. Document the reason for each exclusion so the next campaign does not restart the same investigation.

For implementation detail, connect these rules with the group’s recall list data hygiene process.

### Separate address issues from communication preferences

Create distinct handling rules for an unresolved mailing address, returned mail, a request for an envelope, and a preference for another communication channel. Each situation needs its own next action.

Optometric Management’s [“Bring Back ‘Lost’ Patients”](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2013/may/bring-back-lost-patients) discusses patient segmentation, returned-mail address corrections, campaign ownership, and tracking results within a broader recall process.

Use those operational themes to define who reviews returned mail and how corrected records become eligible for future outreach.

Set an address-review status before the campaign begins. Assign the correction task to an authorized team and document how it returns to the recall workflow. Avoid automatically carrying unresolved records into the next mailing.

Keep working lists within the group’s approved systems. Use aggregate campaign information for leadership reporting, and keep patient-level work with the staff responsible for resolving it.

## Apply communication preferences and approve postcard content

### Use the HHS guidance within its stated scope

HHS states that appointment reminders are “part of treatment” and can be made without HIPAA authorization. That guidance provides a foundation for appointment reminder workflows.

[HHS appointment reminder guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/are-appointment-reminders-allowed-under-hipaa-without-authorization/index.html).

HHS also explains that providers may mail patient reminders and must accommodate reasonable requests for confidential communication.

Its examples include a request for a “closed envelope rather than by postcard.” [HHS guidance on mailed reminders and confidential communications](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/may-health-care-providers-leave-messages/index.html).

Translate that guidance into a release check: review the recorded communication preference and route exceptions to the designated owner before mailing. Have the group’s privacy lead review the proposed content, mailing process, and exception handling.

Keep this review specific to the campaign being approved. If the proposed card includes promotional content or other material beyond the reminder, return it for appropriate review before release.

### Write a restrained message with a clear next action

Ask the content approver to review every visible element of the card, including the reverse side, return address, scheduling instructions, and any printed link or code.

Use a simple message as a starting point:

> Please contact our scheduling team to arrange your next visit. Use the practice contact details shown on this card.

Adapt the final wording through the practice’s approval process. Keep diagnoses, treatment details, insurance information, account balances, and other sensitive content out of the proposed postcard copy.

For a multi-location group, decide whether the card directs responses to a centralized scheduling team or the originating practice. Verify the destination before approving the print file. If the card includes a booking link, review the page patients reach and the location choices it presents.

Maintain an approved template and record who can authorize changes. Include local contact details in that review rather than allowing each location to revise the card independently.

## Connect the mailing to scheduling and follow-up

### Define the outreach sequence and stopping rules

Place postcards within a documented communication sequence. Specify when mail is appropriate, which other approved channels may follow, and what ends outreach for the current recall.

The optometry workflow in [“Bring Back ‘Lost’ Patients”](https://www.optometricmanagement.com/issues/2013/may/bring-back-lost-patients) combines electronic outreach, postcards or letters, and telephone follow-up. It provides an operational example for coordinating channels and assigning campaign responsibility.

For your group, define the status change after each meaningful event. A scheduled appointment should trigger the appropriate update to the active recall queue.

A communication preference change should reach the next person handling outreach. An unresolved scheduling question should remain assigned until it receives a disposition.

Choose follow-up timing through the group’s approved process. Document who decides when outreach pauses, resumes, or moves to a different workflow. Connect the sequence to the broader [front-desk recall workflow](/blog/front-desk-recall-workflow/) so mail responses enter the same operating process as other recall contacts.

### Prepare the team that receives responses

Before releasing the mailing, give scheduling staff the campaign purpose, approved language, eligible locations, and escalation route. Confirm which appointment types they may book and when they must involve the practice.

Prepare for these response categories:

- **Ready to schedule:** Follow the approved location and appointment rules.
- **Already scheduled:** Reconcile the recall record with the existing appointment.
- **Needs a different location:** Apply the group’s cross-location routing rules.
- **Has a clinical question:** Route it to the designated clinical team.
- **Requests different communication:** Record the request through the approved process.
- **Cannot find a suitable appointment:** Assign the capacity exception to an owner.

For callbacks and transferred conversations, specify where staff record the outcome and who owns unfinished work. Review these handoffs alongside the group’s [centralized optometry scheduling standards](/blog/centralized-optometry-scheduling-standards/).

## Evaluate the evidence without assigning unsupported postcard results

### Distinguish combined outreach from postcard effectiveness

A randomized primary eye care study compared usual care with automated or personal calls accompanied by mailed reminder letters.

Its design supports examining mail as part of a broader follow-up process; it did not isolate postcard effectiveness. [“Improving Follow-Up Adherence in a Primary Eye Care Setting”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26656245).

An ophthalmology randomized trial compared standard mailed rescheduling letters with letters plus a patient portal message after missed appointments. The combined-outreach group had higher reattendance during the study’s follow-up period.

That finding concerns letters with portal follow-up in the studied setting. [Ophthalmology reminder trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38403099).

Use these studies to frame evaluation questions about coordinated outreach. Keep the distinction between letters and postcards visible when presenting the evidence to a buying committee. Avoid translating either study into an expected postcard response rate for your group.

### Define what your campaign report can establish

Before launch, decide how a response becomes associated with a campaign. Options to evaluate include a patient mentioning the mailing, a campaign-specific scheduling route, or a documented outreach history. Record the attribution method consistently.

When a patient receives mail and later speaks with a staff member, preserve the sequence in the campaign record. Report the appointment as associated with the recorded outreach according to your definitions. Avoid assigning the postcard sole credit when the workflow involved other contacts.

For comparisons between campaigns, record relevant changes in eligibility, follow-up, appointment availability, and response handling. Give leadership enough context to decide what to adjust. Use observed results from the group’s own workflow rather than importing a response expectation from a different setting.

## Report campaign progress and unresolved work

### Separate mailing activity from patient outcomes

Choose reporting labels that describe the event actually recorded. A print file approved for release, a mailing handed off for fulfillment, a patient response, and a completed visit should have separate statuses.

A practical reporting structure is:

| Reporting category | Definition to establish |
|---|---|
| Eligible for mailing | Passed the approved audience and preference checks |
| Released to mail | Completed the group’s mailing handoff |
| Returned mail | Entered the address-review workflow |
| Response recorded | Generated a documented patient interaction |
| Appointment scheduled | Reconciled with a booking in the scheduling system |
| Visit completed | Confirmed through the approved visit-status process |
| Exception unresolved | Still awaiting an assigned action |

Define the reporting period and the campaign population before reviewing results. Keep leadership reports aggregate and use consistent definitions across locations.

### Review exceptions alongside bookings

Build the review around decisions. Ask which records remain unmailed, which responses remain unresolved, and which locations need a change in scheduling instructions or capacity handling.

Assign an owner and next action to each recurring issue. For example, an address problem belongs in the correction workflow; an unclear appointment type belongs with the designated scheduling or clinical reviewer. Keep those categories distinct so the operations team can address the underlying task.

Optometric Management describes reviewing recall reports monthly and examining issues such as recall triggers and staff understanding of the process.

Use that as a source-supported review example when establishing your group’s cadence. [Recall system monitoring guidance](https://optometricmanagement.com/issues/2021/march/implement-a-patient-recall-system).

Connect the campaign report to the group’s [recall program KPI framework](/blog/recall-program-kpi-healthcare-operators/) so local and enterprise reviews use the same terminology.

## Launch the workflow and evaluate delivery partners

### Compare the complete operating scope

For an internal-versus-outsourced decision, document the work included in each option: list preparation, exception review, template maintenance, mailing coordination, response handling, record updates, and reporting.

Optometric Management recommends considering staff labor, postcards, and postage when comparing internal recall activity with external services. Use that framework to request a clearly scoped proposal. [Recall budgeting framework](https://optometricmanagement.com/issues/2021/march/implement-a-patient-recall-system).

Ask prospective partners to explain who handles returned mail, how scheduling outcomes return to the approved system, and which unresolved tasks remain with the practice. Have the relevant operations, privacy, and technology reviewers assess their parts of the proposed workflow.

Compare proposals against the same responsibilities. Require a clear description of the services, handoffs, reporting, and remaining practice workload before making the operating decision.

### Use a bounded pilot before broader rollout

Choose a pilot scope with clear location ownership and a manageable exception queue. Before releasing mail, approve the audience rules, template, communication preference checks, scheduling route, status definitions, and escalation contacts.

During the pilot, review actual handoffs. Confirm that staff can identify the campaign, reconcile existing appointments, route clinical questions, and assign unresolved responses. Use those observations to revise the playbook before extending it across the group.

Define the rollout decision in advance: which issues must be resolved, who signs off, and how the group will maintain the workflow after launch. Keep clinical decisions with clinical leadership and make operational responsibilities explicit.
